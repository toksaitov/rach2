# `io/`: `poll`, `select`, `epoll`, `eventfd`, `timerfd`, POSIX timers and `inotify`

The `io/` directory has the system calls that wait until one of several descriptors is ready, `poll`, `select` and `epoll` with their signal-mask forms, and four kinds of descriptor that refer to no file, whose readiness those calls report: an eventfd, a 64-bit counter; a timerfd, a timer read through a descriptor; an epoll set; and an inotify instance, a queue of changes to watched files. POSIX timers share the timerfd's timer. Every wait runs in `poll_wait`. The calls follow Linux's ABI on both architectures, for programs such as Dropbear, whose session loop waits in `select`.

### Inside the kernel

* `file_ops.poll` (`fs/file.h`) reports a backing's readiness: the requested `POLLIN` and `POLLOUT` that are true now, `POLLERR` and `POLLHUP` always, and the wait queue whose wake means the answer may have changed, or NULL. `poll_fd` calls it under the big lock unless the table marks it `FILE_OPS_POLL_UNLOCKED`, or all its operations `FILE_OPS_UNLOCKED` (`file_poll_unlocked`): the pipes', the unix sockets', the eventfd's and the timerfd's read their state without it, each word once or under the object's spinlock, after naming their queue, as Linux's poll operations read it after `poll_wait` (fs/pipe.c:656-705, fs/eventfd.c:118-174, fs/timerfd.c:249-263). Without the method, `poll_fd` calls `socket_poll` for a network socket and `tty_poll` for the console, and reports a regular file, a directory, the framebuffer and the mouse ready at once. The rules of each backing are in [the ipc Readme](../ipc/Readme.md) for pipes, [the network Readme](../network/Readme.md) for sockets and poll(2) for the rest.
* A waker passes the kinds of change to `wq_wake`, `WQ_DATA`, `WQ_ROOM` or `WQ_HANGUP`, or all three through `wq_wake_all` for a close or a shutdown (`proc/wait_queue.h`). An epoll registration's record on the queue puts it on its set's ready list only for a wake of a kind its events watch, so a wake of no kind reaches sleepers alone.
* The timer interrupt calls `timerfd_tick` and the interrupt between ticks `timerfd_due` (`kernel.c`) and `sched_account_tick` calls `ptimer_cpu_tick` (`proc/sched.c`); exec (`syscall_proc.c`) calls `ptimer_exec`, signal code that discards signals (`sig_discard`) calls `ptimer_discard`, the exit of a thread (`proc/exit.c`) `ptimer_thread_exit`, and the exit of a process's last thread `ptimer_delete_all`. `syscall.c` has the clocks' part, `clock_now` and `clock_cpu_target`.
* The kernel uses an eventfd as Linux's eventfd_ctx (`eventfd.h`), for KVM's irqfd and ioeventfd and the drivers that follow: `eventfd_get` takes a reference from a descriptor of the caller's and `eventfd_put` drops it; `eventfd_signal` adds 1, from any context, an interrupt handler's among them; and a `struct eventfd_hook` added with `eventfd_hook_add` is told of each rise of the counter, with the counter's spinlock held, and may take the counter as a read would, and of the last close, after it has left the list. `eventfd_hook_remove` takes it off, says whether it was still on the list, and can take the counter with it; a holder that finds it gone leaves the record to the hangup the close has yet to run, since a hangup that sleeps lets other code run before the next hook's. A reference keeps the object after the last close, which marks the counter closed and, under the big lock, takes the hooks off one at a time, each in its own hold of the counter's spinlock, and tells each, so that a remover under the big lock finds its hook on the list or already told; a hook added after that is told of the close at once.
* `struct anon_obj` (`anon.h`) begins the object of every `FD_TYPE_ANON` descriptor: the eventfd, epoll, timerfd and inotify objects, the signalfd (`syscall_proc.c`), a POSIX message queue descriptor (`ipc/mqueue.c`) and a namespace file (`proc/ns.c`). `/proc/PID/fd` prints its name after `anon_inode:`, or alone for a namespace file; a message queue's link is instead `/NAME`, the path of its file in the mqueue file system, with ` (deleted)` once `mq_unlink` has taken the name (`fs/procfs.c`).

## Design

### The wait

`poll_wait` runs a scan function, which fills an array with the wait queue of each object it looked at and returns the number ready, as Linux's `do_poll` (fs/select.c:884-968). Each round clears the thread's `poll_woken`, Linux's `triggered`, by an atomic exchange, a full barrier, and then scans with interrupts on. The wait returns the count if an object is ready, `-EINTR` if a signal that is neither blocked nor ignored is pending, or 0 at the deadline. When the first scan finds nothing, the wait takes a record (`struct wq_waiter`) for each queue from the heap, puts each on its queue with `poll_rec_wake` as its wake function, and scans again; the records stay on their queues until the wait ends, as Linux's poll table entries do. After that, a scan that finds nothing is followed by a sleep (`poll_sleep`), which sets `wakeup_tick` for a finite timeout, marks the thread blocked and calls `schedule` only while `poll_woken` and `sig_woken` are clear, read after the barrier that follows the blocked state, as Linux's `poll_schedule_timeout` (fs/select.c:234-258) and `signal_pending_state` in `__schedule`, and each wake by one scan. A wake calls `poll_rec_wake`, which sets `poll_woken` and makes the thread ready if it is blocked, so a change of readiness before a scan's look is seen by it and one after ends the sleep, and none falls between the scan and the sleep. A scan that names other queues than the records are on, as a descriptor another thread replaced would, moves the records and scans again. The first wait that blocks thus scans twice before it sleeps, where Linux's scan files its entries as it polls (`__pollwait`) and scans once. The test for a signal reads the thread's signal-work flag (`sig_woken`) without a lock and asks `signal_pending`, under the big lock, only when the flag is set, as Linux's `signal_pending` tests `TIF_SIGPENDING` alone; a post sets the flag before it reads the sleeper's state, so a post between the test and the sleep either finds the thread blocked and wakes it or is seen by the sleep. `poll_wait_queue` is the same wait on one queue for a condition, for `epoll_wait`: one exclusive record, filed before the first test of the condition.

A deadline (`struct poll_deadline`) is a tick of the 250 Hz timer and the end of the timeout by time since boot, the `CLOCK_MONOTONIC` reading at the call plus the timeout. `tick_deadline` ends a wait at the first tick at or after its end, counted from where in the tick in progress the call began (`rtc_tick_phase`), so without a lost tick a timeout ends at most a tick late; `deadline_reached` compares it with the 64-bit count (`tick_count`), which does not wrap. A zero timeout ends at that count itself, never at `rtc_tick_phase`'s, which counts a tick the timer has reached and not yet raised, since a deadline a tick ahead of the count would put a zero-timeout call to sleep until that tick, where Linux's returns at once. At that tick `deadline_passed` reads the clock. A tick the timer lost while the processor could not take it leaves the count behind the clock, which keeps its last reading (`rtc_monotonic`), and the deadline then moves to the tick at which the count reaches the end, so a timeout never ends early by the clock. A timeout of 2^40 seconds or more, which `tick_span` cuts, reaches its tick only after that span; its end by time since boot is still recorded, and an end past the largest second stops there, as Linux's does. `select` normalizes the whole seconds in its timeval's microsecond field before validating it and takes the timeout to the microsecond (`tv_deadline`). Each call converts its own timeout and saturates at the limit its manual page gives; on x86-64 `ppoll` and `pselect6` compare their 64-bit seconds with the limit before they narrow them. A wait a signal ends restarts as Linux's does ([signals](../proc/signal.Readme.md)): `poll` files its descriptors and deadline in the restart block (`poll_restart`) and resumes through `restart_syscall` when no handler runs, to its first deadline; `ppoll`, `select`, `_newselect` and `pselect6` end as `ERESTARTNOHAND` and, on every return from a wait with a timeout, write the time left back to the caller's timeout (`poll_finish`), unless the persona has `STICKY_TIMEOUTS` or the copy fails, when an interrupted call returns `EINTR` and is not restarted; the epoll waits are never restarted (`syscall_restartable` in `syscall.c`). `poll_mask_set` installs the mask of `ppoll`, `pselect6`, `epoll_pwait` and `epoll_pwait2`; `poll_mask_done` restores the old one, or, when a signal ended the wait, signal delivery restores it after the handler.

### `poll` and `select`

`poll_scan` sets `revents` to 0 for a negative descriptor and to `POLLNVAL` for one that is not open or was opened with `O_PATH`, and counts the second as ready. It polls any other for its events and `POLLERR` and `POLLHUP`, and masks the answer to those and `POLLNVAL`. `poll` takes at most `FD_TABLE_LIMIT` (1024) entries, or the caller's soft `RLIMIT_NOFILE` when that is higher; Linux takes no more than the limit, but OpenSSH's rlimit sandbox polls under a limit of 0.

`select` is `poll` over a translated array. The i386 wrappers on x86-64 keep the caller's timeout unchanged when the native handler refuses the timeout or signal mask before the wait (`select_left_out` in `arch/x86_64/ia32_fs.c`). `select_common` cuts `nfds` to the caller's table, past which no descriptor is open, but to no fewer than a long's bits, as wide as Linux's table always is, so that a bit for an unopened descriptor in the first long is `EBADF`, as on Linux, and not ignored; it reads and writes the longs that hold the sets' first `nfds` bits, no more, as Linux does, so a program that raised its limit selects a descriptor past `FD_SETSIZE` with sets as wide as it needs; the sets come from the heap. `poll_select.c` turns the three sets into one `pollfd` per selected descriptor, with `POLLIN`, `POLLOUT` and `POLLPRI` for the sets it was in, and writes each result back into those sets alone: `POLLIN`, `POLLHUP` or `POLLERR` as readable, `POLLOUT` or `POLLERR` as writable, `POLLPRI` as exceptional. The scan counts a descriptor ready by the same rule (`pollfd_select_ready`). By `poll`'s rule, a descriptor selected only for writing that reports `POLLHUP` would end the wait with every set empty, and a program that calls `select` in a loop would spin. `poll_select.c` calls no other kernel code, so the host test compiles it unchanged.

### `epoll`

A set (`struct epoll`) has its registrations (`struct epitem`), a ready list, two wait queues, `wq` for the sleepers of `epoll_wait` and `poll_wait` for those that poll the set, `holders`, the registrations of the set in other sets, and `subs`, its registrations of other sets, as Linux's `struct eventpoll`. A registration keeps the number it was added by, the open file description and a copy of the descriptor's entry, through which it is polled, the events, the data word, and its record on the wait queue its target's poll named. The record's wake function, `ep_wake`, Linux's `ep_poll_callback` (fs/eventpoll.c:1429-1534), runs under that queue's lock with interrupts off. For a wake of a kind the registration watches (`kinds_of`: data for the input events, room for the output events, a hangup always) it puts the registration on the set's ready list and wakes one sleeper of `epoll_wait` and every poller of the set. A wake of no kind, which a waker gives for its sleepers alone, changes nothing, so data that comes to a socket watched for output alone puts nothing on the list, as in Linux.

A wait (`epoll_wait_common`), as Linux's `ep_poll`, reports the events of the registrations on the ready list (`send_events`) and while there are none sleeps on `wq` (`poll_wait_queue`). `send_events` takes the set's mutex, `mtx`, takes the whole ready list as a batch under the set's spinlock, `lock`, and then, with interrupts on, polls each registration of the batch again and copies the events of one that is ready to the caller. A level-triggered registration goes back to the tail of the list, so waits with room for fewer events than are ready report the ready descriptors in turn; an edge-triggered one leaves it until a wake puts it back; a one-shot one keeps its flags alone until `EPOLL_CTL_MOD`; and one found not ready leaves the list. A wake that comes while a registration is on a batch marks it, and the batch's end puts it back on the list, as Linux's `ovflist` keeps such wakes. So a wait polls only the registrations that a wake or an earlier report put on the list, never the whole set. An event whose copy faults goes back to the head of the list.

`epoll_wait`, `epoll_pwait`, `epoll_pwait2` and `epoll_ctl` run without the big lock. A registration whose descriptor's poll needs the big lock (`file_poll_unlocked`) is polled under it: a wait takes it before `mtx` when the ready list holds one (`rdbig`), and a wait that meets one without it, put on the list meanwhile, passes it by and, when it reported nothing else, takes the batch again under the big lock. The locks are taken in Linux's order: the big lock, `ep_nest_mtx` (Linux's `epnested_mutex`), each set's `mtx`, an inner set's under its outer set's at the depth it is looked into, and then `ep_files_lock` or a set's `lock`. `epoll_ctl` takes the set's `mtx`, and `ep_nest_mtx` before it when the descriptor is a set. An add polls the target, puts the record on the queue the poll named and polls once more, so that a change before the record was there is seen. `EPOLLEXCLUSIVE` makes the record an exclusive entry of its queue: a wake calls the exclusive records in turn until one wakes a sleeper of its set for an event it watches, as Linux's `ewake`, so of the sets with such a registration and a sleeper, one is woken. `EPOLL_CTL_MOD` with `EPOLLEXCLUSIVE`, and `EPOLLEXCLUSIVE` on a set or with an event other than `EPOLLIN`, `EPOLLOUT`, `EPOLLERR`, `EPOLLHUP`, `EPOLLWAKEUP` and `EPOLLET`, are `EINVAL`, as in Linux.

A registration lasts until it is removed or until the last close of its open file description, whatever became of the number it was added by, as in Linux. The release of a description's last descriptor (`fd_release_objects` in `syscall_fs.c`) calls `epoll_release_file`, Linux's `eventpoll_release_file`, before what the description names is released, and that takes each registration on the description's list (`epolls`) from its set under the set's `mtx`. A registration's queue thus outlasts its record, but for a thread group's signalfd queue, which goes with the group: `wq_wake_free`, Linux's `wake_up_pollfree`, takes every record off it first. A set's last close (`epoll_close`) removes its registrations, but for those a release is taking, and the set goes with the last of its references, which its description and each registration hold, as Linux's `ep->refcount`. A wait's descriptor keeps the set's description open while it runs.

Sets nest. A set is readable while a wait on it would report an event (`set_poll`, Linux's `__ep_eventpoll_poll`): under the set's `mtx`, which a wait holds while its batch has the list, so that an outer set never finds the list empty while a wait has it, the registrations on its ready list are polled until one is ready, those that are not leave it, and a set inside is looked into at most `EP_MAX_NESTS` (4) deep. A wake that puts a registration on a set's list wakes the set's `poll_wait`, where `poll`, `select` and the registrations of the set in other sets have their records, one nesting deeper for the lock checker. `EPOLL_CTL_ADD` of a set fails with `ELOOP` when it would make a cycle or a chain of more than five sets (`nests_badly`), counted through every registration that lasts, whichever process made it, under `ep_nest_mtx`.

### `eventfd`

An eventfd is a 64-bit counter and a wait queue. A read takes the value, the whole counter or 1 with `EFD_SEMAPHORE`, and wakes the writers before it copies the value to the caller, so a second reader during a copy that sleeps in a page fault finds it gone; a failed copy loses the value, as in Linux. A read wakes the queue for room, and a write of a nonzero value for data. The counter's spinlock covers each change of the counter, the list of hooks and the references, since a kernel signal can come from an interrupt handler and a holder may take or drop a reference with or without the big lock; a read or a write tests the counter, and chooses to wait or to fail with `EAGAIN`, in one hold of that lock, and its wait's condition reads the counter under it, since a 64-bit word is two loads on i686. Only a kernel signal carries the counter past 0xfffffffffffffffe, to 0xffffffffffffffff, where every write waits until a read and poll reports `POLLERR`, as in Linux. Read, write, poll and the last close run without the big lock (`FILE_OPS_UNLOCKED`), as Linux's take only the counter's wait-queue lock (fs/eventfd.c:109-116, 214-280), so a write that ends another thread's epoll wait waits for no other processor's hold; the close takes the big lock only to tell the hooks, whose hangups expect it. `sys_read` sends a zero-byte read of an anonymous descriptor to its read method (`syscall_fs.c`), so a zero-byte read of an eventfd or a timerfd fails with `EINVAL`, as a short one does.

### Timers

`struct ktimer` is the timer of a timerfd and of a POSIX timer. An armed timer is on `armed`, which `timerfd_tick` walks at each tick and `timerfd_due` at each interrupt between ticks, or, on a clock of processor time, on `cpu_armed`, which `ptimer_cpu_tick` walks after it adds the tick to the thread's times, under `timer_lock`. A timerfd last set with `TFD_TIMER_ABSTIME` and `TFD_TIMER_CANCEL_ON_SET` on `CLOCK_REALTIME` is on `cancelable`, as on Linux's cancel_list. `timer_lock`, a spinlock taken with interrupts off, by the tick without the big lock and by a system call after it, and before the clock's own lock, covers the three lists, each timer's count, arming and cancellation, each process's list of POSIX timers and the list of timers whose signal is due.

A timer of the clocks keeps its time, `end`, in nanoseconds on its clock, `base`, and its interval, `ins`, in nanoseconds, as Linux's timerfd_setup keeps them; a relative timer of `CLOCK_REALTIME` is based on `CLOCK_MONOTONIC`, as Linux's hrtimer bases one, and a relative time or an interval past `TIME_MAX` seconds (4294967295, about 136 years) is taken as that. `due` is the first tick at or after `end`, which `rtc_tick_phase` places within the tick in progress, as a sleep ends, and which `timerfd_tick` compares with the count. The clock and the tick are read together with interrupts off under `timer_lock` (`read_now`), so that no tick comes between them and the time to `end` counts from the tick it is added to. At that tick the timer reads its clock: short of `end`, as when the clock runs ahead of the tick count, it only moves `due` on, as `deadline_passed` in poll.c does, so it never expires early. A timer has the timer's interrupt between ticks armed for its `end` however far it is (`arm_between`), as Linux starts an hrtimer when it is set, at `end` itself on the monotonic clock and at `end` less the real-time clock's offset (`rtc_offset`) on `CLOCK_REALTIME`, as Linux programs an hrtimer at its expiry less its base's offset, so that no time passed between the reading and the arming is added: `set_due` arms it when the timer is set or moved on, and `timerfd_tick` again at each tick, since a processor keeps only the earliest time asked of it. That interrupt calls `timerfd_due`, which compares each such timer's `end` with one reading of its base's clock for the pass, as Linux's hrtimer interrupt compares every timer with one reading, so the timer expires at its time, as Linux's hrtimer does, and a timer whose time is still to come is armed again. `timerfd_tick` likewise reads each base's clock once for its pass. Each pass expires the timers it finds due in the order Linux runs them, `CLOCK_MONOTONIC`'s, then `CLOCK_REALTIME`'s, then `CLOCK_BOOTTIME`'s, each base's earliest end first (`expires_first`), as __hrtimer_run_queues does (kernel/time/hrtimer.c:71-87, 1791-1811), so that POSIX timers' signals are posted, or wait, in the order the timers expired. Equal ends expire in the order of the most recent arming or rearming: a delivery that rearms a periodic timer moves it first on `armed` (`requeue_clock`), behind those already queued for that end, as Linux 6.12.115's hrtimer_restart and timerqueue_add (kernel/time/posix-timers.c:244-251, lib/timerqueue.c:19-37, include/linux/rbtree.h:172-180); so does the post of an ignored signal, which Linux's callback rearms directly (posix-timers.c:341-374). Counting intervals while a signal is pending does not change that order. A reader's wake is late by the time since `end` or since `end` was last counted (`set_at`), whichever is later, so that a timer set to a time past, or passed by a step of the clock, counts only the time since; the clock is read for it at the wake, not taken from the pass's reading, which the timers before it in the pass share. A POSIX timer is armed between ticks only while `between` says: when set, unless it is `SIGEV_NONE`'s, as Linux never starts that hrtimer; again at an expiration that sets its signal pending; and again once that signal is taken (`ptimer_rearm`, from the signal's dequeue), as Linux's posixtimer_rearm restarts a timer at the delivery of its signal, with `end` moved past the clock, as posixtimer_rearm forwards the timer. An expiration while the timer's signal is pending clears it, and the ticks alone then count the timer, so a short interval cannot raise interrupt after interrupt between ticks. An expiration whose signal the process ignores clears it only for an interval under a tick, as Linux restarts an ignored timer at once and moves it a tick on only when its interval is shorter. An expiration that waits for another sender's pending signal of the same number clears it too, and the dequeue of that signal posts the waiting expirations and sets it again when that sets the timer's signal pending, since Linux queues a signal for each timer and restarts each at its own. `timer_gettime` of a periodic timer the ticks alone count first expires it against its clock (`expire`), counting the intervals in `set_count`, so its time left is never one passed, as Linux's common_timer_get forwards such a timer; the time left counts from the same reading, as common_timer_get counts both from one `now`, and `timerfd_gettime` likewise counts it from the reading `timerfd_forward` moved the timer by. Past `end`, it counts its expiration and wakes its readers, each reader's wake record begun under the run queues' lock, taken after the queue's, so that a wake from elsewhere does not overwrite it; or, for a POSIX timer, it joins the list of timers whose signal is due (`ptimer_fire_later`), once however many expirations come meanwhile, since a pass runs without the big lock and a signal is posted under it, and is left to the ticks until then. The interrupt between ticks, which runs without the big lock, posts them at once when a try takes it, and otherwise leaves them to a tick; a tick's locked part, or a later tick that takes the lock, posts the tick's (`ptimer_post_due`, `timer_lock_take` in `kernel.c`), as Linux posts a timer's signal from its callback (kernel/time/posix-timers.c:318-377). A `timer_settime` or a `timer_delete` posts one still due before it changes the timer (`ptimer_post_one`), as Linux sent it at expiry, whether its expiration is the setting's first or one after the setting's last signal was taken, and drops the expirations that joined it, since Linux stops the timer once its signal is queued (kernel/time/posix-timers.c:318-377); exec drops it with the timer. A one-shot timer is disarmed. A periodic POSIX timer also counts every interval that has ended since, as Linux's hrtimer_forward does, and moves `end` on by as many intervals. A periodic timerfd stops at its expiry instead, armed but off the list with `due` at its largest and `expired` set, as Linux's timerfd_tmrproc returns HRTIMER_NORESTART (fs/timerfd.c:58-80); a read that takes the count, `timerfd_gettime`, or a setting starts it again, and the first two move `end` on past the clock as it reads then and add the intervals moved less one to the count (`timerfd_forward`), as Linux's hrtimer_forward_now and hrtimer_restart do (fs/timerfd.c:292-313, 552-565). After a step of the clock back before `end`, nothing moves and the count loses the expiration, as in Linux, so the timer is due again at the time it stopped at; a read left with no count then returns `EAGAIN`, or 0 when it blocks, as Linux's does. `timerfd_gettime` reports both to the nanosecond. The kernel has no 64-bit division of its own, so `div64` divides by shifts. A timer of processor time keeps its time left, `cpu_left`, and its interval, `ins`, in nanoseconds, and each tick charged to it takes a tick's length off; at the tick that uses the time up a periodic one is armed again for its interval less the part of the tick past the expiry, each whole interval that part covers counted as an expiration more, as Linux's bump_cpu_timer moves the expiry on (kernel/time/posix-cpu-timers.c:122-147) (`cpu_timer_charge`). A setting reads the clock of the process or thread the timer was made for (`clock_cpu_sample`), not the caller's, and arms the timer, under `timer_lock`, which the tick adds to the times under (`ptimer_cpu_tick`), so that a tick between the reading and the arming cannot pass unseen, as Linux's tick compares the clock with the timer's absolute expiry. ### `inotify`

An instance (`struct inotify`) has a queue of events and a wait queue. Every watch of the machine is on one list (`watches`) and is found by the base mount of its file system (`vfs_mount_base`) and the inode number, a key an open file description has too, so no hook needs a path. `inotify_watches` counts the watches, and every path to a hook tests it first, so a machine with no watch pays one comparison per call. Each base mount counts the watches on its file system too (`inotify_watched`, kept by `vfs_mount_watches`), and the descriptor calls test that word for the description's file (`inotify_mount_watched`), as Linux's `fsnotify_sb_has_watchers` tests a superblock before any event (include/linux/fsnotify.h:34-37, 81): a read, an open or a last close of a file on a file system with no watch takes no big lock for inotify and records no name, though another file system has watches. `fs/vfs.c` reports what its namespace operations and `vfs_setattr` change (`inotify_child`, `inotify_file`, `inotify_gone`), and the descriptor calls in `syscall_fs.c` and `fs/fd.c` report reads, writes, truncations, opens and last closes through the open file description (`inotify_desc`). While a watch is on the file's file system, `open_done` records in the description the directory and last component of the name it was opened by (`open_record_parent`), so the directory's watches hear of those events under that name. The calls that change attributes, `chmod`, `chown` and `utimensat` in each form, tell the directory's watches themselves, since `vfs_setattr` has no name: through a path, `attr_told` finds the directory and the last component again (`named_parent`) and reports to it when that component is still the file; through a descriptor, `attr_told_desc` uses what the open recorded. A chown that names an owner or a group is `IN_ATTRIB` even when the file has them already, as on Linux. On the KRFFS boot root, whose changes do not pass through `vfs_setattr`, the same two tell the file's own watches as well.

`queue_event` drops an event equal to the last unread one, as Linux merges it. Past `inotify_max_queued_events` (16384), or when the heap has no room, it queues the one `IN_Q_OVERFLOW` record kept in the instance, so the overflow needs no memory. A read takes each record off the queue before it copies it. An unmount gives each watch on the mount `IN_UNMOUNT` and `IN_IGNORED` and frees it (`inotify_unmount`), since a watch keeps no reference to its mount. A user may have `inotify_max_user_instances` (128) instances and `inotify_max_user_watches` (8192) watches. The three limits start at Linux's defaults and are the files of `/proc/sys/fs/inotify`, which a privileged write sets; each is read where it applies, so a lower value refuses the next instance, watch or event and takes nothing away.

## Limits

* No backing reports `POLLPRI`, so a wait for it alone ends only at its timeout or a signal.
* A node of `/dev` or `/proc` can be watched with inotify and reports nothing.

## Tests

`tests/run host` compiles `poll_select_hosttest.c`, `poll_deadline_hosttest.c`, `epoll_hosttest.c`, `eventfd_hosttest.c`, `timerfd_hosttest.c` and `wait_queue_hosttest.c` from `tests/host/` natively under AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer. [The host tests' Readme](../../../tests/host/Readme.md) gives their rows.

`tests/run default`, in the core tier, runs these on both architectures:

* `tests/pollprobe.c`: `poll` and `select` over pipes: a timeout, `POLLIN`, `POLLHUP`, `_newselect`, a child's write waking a poller, `select`'s errors, on x86-64 a `ppoll` of 2^32 seconds, and the console.
* `tests/musl/pselectmusl.c`: `pselect6`'s three sets, a timeout, its errors, and its signal mask: a pending signal the mask unblocks ends the wait and its handler runs under the mask, a signal the mask blocks stays pending, a signal sent while the call sleeps ends it, and the mask before the call is back after each.
* `tests/musl/epollmusl.c`: the eventfd counter; epoll's level-triggered, edge-triggered and one-shot reports, nesting and `ELOOP`, a fork's child that closed a nested set's number, reports in turn, an event a wait could not store, and the edges of AF_UNIX, pipe, UDP, TCP and pseudoterminal wakes.
* `tests/musl/timerfdmusl.c`: reads, intervals, a disarmed timer, an absolute time already past, a timer waking an epoll wait, and settings of 200 days.
* `tests/musl/inotifymusl.c`: the events of a watched directory and a watched file through a file's life, reads, `FIONREAD`, `poll`, and the errors and flags of the watch calls.
* `tests/musl/callsmusl.c`: a POSIX timer's signal and overrun, a deleted id, and timers on clocks of processor time.

The `packet`, `raw`, `netif` and `dhcp` suites poll sockets. `tests/musl/pollmusl.c` is staged as `/musl/pollmusl` for a person to run; no suite runs it.
