# `usb/`: the USB stack

Rach2 drives USB keyboards, mice, flash drives and hubs. The stack is a host-controller driver, a device core, and three class drivers, HID, hub and mass storage, and only the first of them touches a register.

## Why xHCI, and only xHCI

There are four host-controller interfaces, and this kernel implements the newest. UHCI, OHCI and EHCI are on no machine this project runs on except QEMU, and xHCI is on both: QEMU offers `qemu-xhci`, and the notebook has an Intel Lynx Point-LP xHCI at `00:14.0`. A controller of another interface is named at boot, `usb: unsupported: ehci at 00:1d.0`, and not driven.

It is also the only one that reaches a low-speed keyboard without a second mechanism. On xHCI the controller handles low, full, high and SuperSpeed alike. On the notebook's EHCI a low-speed device is reachable only through the chipset's integrated rate-matching hub, which would cost a hub class driver and split transactions to arrive where xHCI arrives directly.

## The layers

| File | What it owns |
|------|--------------|

| `xhci.c` | The controller: registers, rings, contexts, commands, transfers. |
| `core.c` | Devices, descriptors, the enumeration state machine, class matching. |
| `hid.c` | The boot-protocol keyboard and mouse. |
| `msc.c` | The flash drive, over Bulk-Only Transport. |
| `hub.c` | The hub: its descriptor, its ports' power, and the port operations for the devices below it. |
| `usb.c` | The inventory, the bring-up, and the stack's turn from the timer. |

## Every controller, each on its own

Every xHCI controller on the bus is driven, up to four of them. Discovery walks the whole bus and takes a record for each, `struct xhci_ctrl` in `xhci.c`, holding that controller's aperture, capability record, rings, context array, port state, slots, completions and the command in flight. Nothing is shared between them: two controllers run beside each other, each with its own interrupt vector, and a controller whose bring-up fails is left out with its reason while the others come up. `xhci_controllers`, `xhci_controller` and `xhci_number` are how the rest of the kernel reaches them, and every call about a controller takes the record. A machine with more than four is told so once, and each controller past the fourth is named at its address and not driven, since the walk goes on to the end of the bus.

The tables are the controller's own size. The port state is one entry per port it reports, the slot table and the context array are its own slot count, and it is told to enable every slot it offers rather than a number compiled here. A slot's device context and each endpoint's ring are one page each, taken when the slot is addressed and when the endpoint is opened, and given back when the slot closes. Those pages come from the heap, which is not identity-mapped, so every address written for the controller to read is the physical one recorded beside the address this kernel reaches the page by. The scratchpads are as many as the controller asks for; the one page holding the array of their addresses is the limit, 512 of them, and a controller asking for more is refused by name.

A completion carries the controller it came from. Slot numbers start at one on every controller, so a machine with two of them has two devices called slot one, and a driver that matched a completion by slot alone would hand one controller's answer to the other's device.

A device's record and a hub's record are each a frame from the identity-mapped window, taken when the device appears and given back when it goes. The controller writes a device's descriptors and its reports straight into that record, so the record must be memory whose two addresses are the same. That is also what makes room to read a configuration whole: the whole descriptor is read, and one longer than the record's buffer is refused by name rather than truncated to its header and the device driven by half its description.

A device that asks for a slot and is told there are none does not fail. It keeps its entry and its reset and asks again when a slot comes free, which is what unplugging another device does.

Addressing belongs to the controller rather than the core: xHCI's Address Device command replaces the `SET_ADDRESS` request, so the core asks the driver to address a device and never sends that request itself. A second controller whose bus really takes the request would send it inside its own `device_address`.

A device is opened at a location, `struct usb_location` in `hcd.h`: the root port it is under, how many hubs are above it, the route string, and, for a low or full-speed device below a high-speed hub, the slot and port of the hub that translates for it and whether that hub was set to translate per port. The core fills it in and `xhci.c` writes it into the slot context: the route string in the first word's low twenty bits, four for each tier, with the port on the hub nearest the root in the lowest nibble and each deeper hub's port in the next, which is the specification's order. `xhci_device_open` returns an error for a depth past five tiers or a route with bits above its depth, since a device addressed by a wrong port is one the controller would talk to at random. A port number above fifteen does not fit a nibble, and the specification writes fifteen in its place: below a full- or high-speed hub that loses nothing, because the packet is routed by the translator's own port number, which has eight bits and carries the real one. That is allowed only where a translator names the port, so a device that deep under a SuperSpeed hub, which is routed by the string alone, is refused instead. `xhci_device_hub` writes into a hub's own slot context, through the evaluate command, its port count and, for a high-speed hub, whether it translates per port and its think time; a device below such a hub has the hub's slot and port in its own third word and the multi-TT bit in its first, and the controller issues the split transactions itself, so this kernel writes none. The host test checks the words against the specification; whether a controller schedules from them is the notebook's proof.

## The one pump, and the two contexts

The event ring is drained by one function, `usb_pump` in `usb.c`, and by nothing else. It runs from the controller's interrupt and from the 100 Hz timer, which calls it every tick whether or not an interrupt arrived. To enable messages, the driver calls `isr_alloc_vector` for the lowest unallocated vector from 48 through 127. On success the boot log reports `usb: interrupts: messages on vector N`, and `/proc/interrupts` identifies the handler as `xhci` once an interrupt has arrived. If messages cannot be enabled, the driver tries the controller's PCI interrupt line; the timer remains active in either case.

Whichever turns it, the interrupt flag stays as that context found it. The handler runs from an interrupt gate and the timer's handler does too, and the pump saves and restores the flag rather than enabling interrupts on its way out. That is what keeps the two from being inside the ring at once on this one processor. A pump that returns with the flag raised records `usb: pump: interrupts were enabled inside it`, once.

The handler acknowledges the interrupt before it drains, and the two are one call in the driver (`xhci_interrupt_take`). In the other order, an acknowledgement after the drain would clear the pending bit of an event written in between, and that event would wait for the timer with no message coming for it. The dequeue pointer is written back with the event-handler-busy bit as one, and while the interrupter is on it is written on an empty drain as well: the controller sets that bit when it raises an interrupt and raises no other until it is written back, so a handler that found the ring already drained by the timer would otherwise silence the controller for good.

The timer is the fallback for a lost edge, so a missed message costs at most ten milliseconds and never a hang. How often it finds work that no message was coming for is counted and reported as `FBK` in `/proc/interrupts`. Whether a message was coming is asked of the processor rather than of the controller: the controller's own pending bit is set whether or not a message went out, while a message in the processor's request or in-service register is one this tick's interrupt flag has held back, which is a race rather than a lost edge. It is asked once after the drain and once at the end of the pump, because the event reaches memory before the message reaches the processor. Under the emulator the count is zero; on hardware a small count beside the vector's own is that same race.

`rach2.nomsi`, a processor whose APIC was refused, and a controller with no MSI capability all leave messages off; the controller then raises its interrupts on the PCI line the firmware routed for it, through the 8259, and `/proc/interrupts` lists that line as `PIC xhci` at its own vector, IRQ 7 on the notebook and IRQ 11 under the emulator. The line is level-triggered, so the handler's acknowledgement clears `USBSTS.EINT` as well as `IMAN.IP`; a controller that names no line is left to the timer alone, which on the notebook read a stick at 103 KB/s where the line reads it at the rate messages do. `rach2.msidrop=N` silences the controller after N messages, which no hardware switch can do on demand, and is how the fallback is seen to work.

With `rach2.usblatency` on the boot line the kernel stamps four points of a keystroke's path and prints them when a reader takes the byte; without it the stamps are taken and nothing is printed, since on a console the record is a line for every key typed. The four: the handler's entry, the drain that took the event, the key reaching the line discipline, and the read. The record is `usb: latency: msi 1, entry -5, wake 5, read 79, us from the drain`. The drain is the base because it is the first moment this stack knows the event exists; the controller stamps nothing, so the time it wrote the event is not a number any of this can report. The last of the four includes whatever the reading program was doing when the key arrived; the first three are this kernel's own. There is one set of stamps and not one per byte, so a burst of keys taken by one read prints the last of them.

## Nothing waits

Nothing on the pump's path may wait: not a port reset, not a control transfer, not a command completion, not a millisecond delay. So `submit` queues work and returns, `poll` reports what finished, and enumeration is a state machine that advances at most one step per call. Each device has its own state and a deadline counted in milliseconds; a step that does not complete by its deadline fails the device and gives the slot back. Every step gets half a second except the configuration request, which gets five, as the Linux driver allows it: the notebook's stick behind the hub, present at boot, answered it after the half second while its flash started.

At boot the kernel waits up to 400 ms while the timer drives that machine, so the devices present at reset are enumerated before the first process starts where they answer in time; a slower one, or the devices behind a hub still reading its descriptor, are finished by the timer's pump afterwards. The boot path does not drive it itself: two parties draining one event ring, one of them inside an interrupt of the other, is the defect this arrangement exists to prevent. The controller's own interrupt is turned on last, after the devices present at reset are enumerated, so that no message arrives at a stack that is still building its rings.

One command is in flight at a time, and one device is enumerated at a time. The controller has one command ring; a second device waits at its port, which costs a hundred milliseconds. A device takes the turn only when the ring is free, and a command past its second is aborted from the pump's clock whether or not the device that issued it still waits: the notebook's soldered port 7 once never answered an Address Device, the device was given up on at its half second, and the command left running held the ring against every device after it until the boot had lost them all.

The only waits in the driver are in bring-up, which runs once: the reset, the firmware handoff, and the fixed millisecond an Intel controller needs after its reset is asserted before its registers may be read again.

## What the controller is asked for

`CAPLENGTH`, the interface version, the slot, interrupter and port counts, the scratchpad count, the context size, the addressing width, the register offsets and the supported page sizes are all read rather than assumed: the two machines this kernel runs on differ in several of them, and a driver that guessed any one would work on one machine and corrupt memory on the other.

A controller is refused, with a record saying which, when its aperture is a 64-bit window above 4 GiB, when its registers do not fit the 64 KiB this driver maps, when it does not offer 4 KiB pages, when its capability list leaves the aperture, when it never halts or never resets or never says it is ready, when the firmware never gives it up, or when it asks for more scratchpad pages than this kernel compiles in.

## The ports the chipset will give

An Intel chipset routes each USB2 pin to either this controller or the companion EHCI, and the firmware decides at power-on. It leaves some on the companion so that an operating system with no xHCI driver still has working sockets. This kernel has no EHCI driver, so a pin left there is a socket nothing can reach: on the notebook this milestone was written for, the firmware gave this controller two of nine, and the seven it kept were the two sockets a person plugs into.

So bring-up moves them. `XUSB2PR` at configuration offset `0xD0` takes the pins `XUSB2PRM` at `0xD4` says may move, and the SuperSpeed pair at `0xD8` and `0xDC` works the same way. The mask is read rather than assumed, and only an Intel controller is written to at all. The routing is the firmware's again after the next power cycle, so this runs on every boot, and a machine on which it goes wrong is one power cycle from the way it was. It is what every other operating system on this hardware does at the same point.

## A device this kernel cannot drive

A machine has USB devices soldered into it: a Bluetooth radio, a card reader, a camera. None of them offers a boot-protocol keyboard or mouse, so each is refused, and the refusal has to be remembered or the device is enumerated again the moment its port is looked at.

It is remembered by port, in a word of bits, and not by holding an entry in the device table. A notebook with three such devices would otherwise spend three of its four entries on them, leaving one for everything a person plugs in: unplug once and the freed entry goes to another soldered device, after which nothing plugged in is enumerated again until the machine reboots. A bit is cleared when its port says the device has gone, so a device refused once is tried again when it is plugged in again.

## The fixed limits

| Limit | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Device slots the controller is asked for | 16, or fewer on a controller that offers fewer, numbered from one; the device table has sixteen entries, shared by hubs, leaf devices, devices being enumerated and devices being closed; the notebook's four soldered devices, a USB 3 hub's two halves, a keyboard, a mouse and a stick are nine |
| Endpoint numbers reachable on a device | 0 to 15, which is thirty-one directions; a boot device uses two and a stick two |
| Controllers driven | 4; each one past that is named at its address and not driven |
| Root ports reached | 16 |
| Scratchpad pages given | 32 |
| Keyboards and mice driven at once | as many as the slots hold, with one modifier state between them |
| Flash drives driven at once | 8 class-driver devices, with up to 4 logical units each; the USB disk layer has 8 unit records, and the registry has 8 disk records shared with ATA and AHCI |
| Hubs driven at once | 2, each with up to 15 ports used |
| Tree depth | 2 hubs, the hub driver's table; the route string could say 5 |
| Requests waiting on one stick | 16, the request pool's own size |
| A stick's request deadline | ten seconds, in the block layer and in the driver alike |

Each is a table in the kernel image. A controller with more root ports than this is used as far as sixteen and says so; one that requires more scratchpad pages is refused with both numbers in the record.

The modifier state is shared rather than one per keyboard. Two keyboards plugged in at once would share a shift: releasing it on either releases it for both. That is the cost of one input path, and it is the one place where the two keyboards are not independent.

## Boot protocol, and no report descriptors

A human-interface device describes its reports in a language of its own, and reading that language is the largest part of a USB HID stack. Every firmware skips it the same way this does: a keyboard and a mouse must offer a boot protocol, whose two report layouts are fixed. A device that offers no boot protocol is refused.

The cost is that a device whose interface is not class 3, subclass 1, protocol 1 or 2 is not driven, whatever else it can do.

## From a report to a keystroke

A HID report is a state and a scancode is an event. Each report is compared with the one before it: a usage that appeared is a make code, one that went away is a break code, and one in both sends nothing. The codes are set-1, which is what the PS/2 keyboard produces, so no code downstream has to tell the two kinds of keyboard apart. The modifier byte is turned the same way, with the right-hand modifiers in their two-byte extended form.

A report whose key slots hold an error usage is the keyboard saying it has lost count of its own keys. Its modifier byte is still good and is read; its key array is dropped, so what is really held stays held.

A device that goes releases everything it was holding. The release that would have lifted a held key is on a device that is no longer there, so this driver sends it.

A mouse report becomes a packet in the queue the PS/2 mouse fills, with the vertical movement turned over: a USB mouse's Y grows downward and that queue's grows upward.

## Mass storage

A flash drive is a SCSI target reached through two bulk endpoints. Every command is two or three transfers: a 31-byte command block wrapper out, the data in or out when the command has any, and a 13-byte status wrapper in. `msc.c` is a state machine over those three, stepped from the same pump the timer and the interrupt share, with one command in flight per stick.

Each published block device has its unit record as `drvdata`. `msc_lun_device(index, lun)` and `msc_lun_ident(index, lun, out)` select a unit; `msc_block_device(index)` and `msc_ident(index, out)` select unit zero. Units with at least 2^32 sectors use `READ(16)` and `WRITE(16)`; smaller units use `READ(10)` and `WRITE(10)`. All units of a device share its bulk endpoints and sixteen-entry request queue; a request is at most 1024 sectors, 512 KiB, one command with one data transfer of up to 129 blocks. Requests are served one at a time in arrival order, with the command wrapper's LUN field set from the request's unit. The request pool admits at most sixteen live requests in total and normally at most eight per submitting block device, so it cannot admit enough requests to overflow this queue.

Bring-up begins with `Get Max LUN`; a stall or an answer above 15 selects unit zero alone. The driver examines units from zero through the reported maximum, up to four units per device. Each gets `INQUIRY`, which must report a direct-access disk and supplies the vendor and product strings for `/proc/usb`; `TEST UNIT READY`, retried for up to ten seconds per unit with `REQUEST SENSE` after each failure; `READ CAPACITY(10)`; and `MODE SENSE(6)`. A failed INQUIRY, a non-disk unit, no medium, or a block size other than 512 bytes is recorded and passed over. A readiness timeout or failed capacity command refuses the device. The capacity field is the last block address, so the sector count is one greater; `0xFFFFFFFF` selects `READ CAPACITY(16)`, service action `0x10` of command `0x9E`, with a 32-byte answer. An all-ones 64-bit last address is refused. The MODE SENSE write-protect bit makes every mount of that unit read-only; refusal of MODE SENSE means no write protection was reported. After the last unit, the driver publishes each accepted unit as a block device; if none was accepted, it refuses the device.

Every way a command can end has one answer. A data stage that stalls has the halt cleared on that endpoint and the status wrapper read; a status wrapper that stalls has its endpoint cleared and is read again, once. A wrapper the stick will not take, a status wrapper that stalls a second time, one that arrives with the wrong tag or the wrong signature or a status this class does not define, a residue larger than the transfer, and a phase error each lead to the full recovery, whose order the specification fixes: the class reset, then the halt cleared on the inward endpoint, then on the outward one. A check condition is followed by `REQUEST SENSE`, and the driver branches on the sense key: a unit attention, which a stick reports after a reset or a change of medium, has the command sent once more; an illegal request in answer to `SYNCHRONIZE CACHE` is a stick with no cache and not a failure; any other key fails the request with `EIO`. A read or write of up to 1024 sectors is one command, its pages sent as one transfer descriptor, and `/proc/disk` counts each such command. The request's pages are the controller's from the data transfer's doorbell until the controller driver reports the transfer retired; a transfer the controller has not retired when its status wrapper completes, which is every short transfer under QEMU since it posts no event for the last block, is retired by stopping its endpoint and moving the ring on through the core before the request is completed and before the next command, `REQUEST SENSE` included. A transfer that never answers is not completed at its deadline: the inward endpoint is stopped and moved on, then the outward one, then the full recovery runs, and the request is completed when recovery ends. Each halt cleared in a recovery is two halves: the controller's endpoint reset, or stopped, and moved on through the core, then the device's halt cleared with a standard `CLEAR_FEATURE(ENDPOINT_HALT)` request. When a stop, a dequeue, a clear or the class reset fails or is not answered within its deadline, the driver stops serving the stick, keeps the request until the slot is given back, and asks the core to close the device, whose port reset enumerates it again. A `Get Max LUN` the stick stalls halts the control endpoint, which is reset and moved on the same way before the next transfer on it. The block layer's own deadline is the same ten seconds, so the process waiting is woken with `EIO` at that deadline and the request's page stays quarantined until the driver's late completion frees it, which is what the quarantine is for. Each command wrapper has a tag that goes up across every stick and every attach, so a completion left over from a stick that was pulled cannot match a command of the one plugged in after it. The stick's clearing of a halt goes through the core, because the controller takes one command at a time for the whole stack.

The block device the stick is published as has `submit` and `flush` and not `read` and `write`: a transfer to a stick completes later, from the pump, so the device cannot be used at boot or with interrupts off. A process reads it through the block layer's sleeping path, and the disk layer reads its partition table from the pump through `block_request_begin`, which starts one request and is asked after on later passes. Those two are the only entries from a process, and they run with interrupts masked: everything else that touches a stick runs in the pump, and the emulator completes a transfer inside the doorbell write, so the interrupt for a wrapper can arrive before the stage that sent it has been recorded. Requests are admitted in the order they arrived and served one at a time, since two processes can each be asleep inside a request on the one stick; the next one waiting goes out at the end of every pump pass, after a command ended and after a timeout alike, so nothing submitted in between overtakes it. A flush waits its turn like a read, which is what puts it after every write before it. A transfer that never answers is failed after the stops and its transport recovery, the mass-storage reset and the two halts cleared; a recovery that never answers either leaves the request kept at its own deadline, and the driver asks the core to close the device, which fails the request when the slot is given back and enumerates the port again through a port reset, as a replug by hand would. The notebook's stick behind the SuperSpeed hub stopped answering after 33 MB of a FAT32 write with the hub reporting its link up, which is the case. `flush` is `SYNCHRONIZE CACHE`; the mounts call it at an unmount, a sync and an fsync, and the stick's line in `/proc/usb` counts the ones it answered.

A stick that is pulled out is a port that says so, which the core already sees. Every request waiting is failed and every later one is refused; a request whose data transfer is in flight is failed when the controller has given the slot back, since its pages are the controller's until then, and a completion that arrives after that is told apart by the generation each submission carries. The stick's record goes when the disk layer has released it and the controller has given the slot back, in either order; a slot the controller never gives back keeps the record, the request and its pages, with a line in the log. A process asleep in a read returns with `EIO` rather than waiting for a device that will not answer. The slot is not freed then: its block device is what a mount, an open description and a held buffer all point at, and a stick plugged in afterwards would be handed those pointers with the slot. The disk layer counts what still holds the stick and frees the slot when the count reaches zero; [`block/Readme.md`](../block/Readme.md) has that count and what it covers.

## Hubs

A port belongs to the root controller or to a hub, and the core's enumeration does not care which: a device record has a pointer to its port's four operations, status, settled, reset begun and reset done, and the core calls the controller's set for a root port and the hub driver's set for a hub port. The debounce, the reset, the addressing and the descriptors are the same code for both. A device below a hub is opened at a location built from the hub's: the hub's route with the port, numbered from one, in the nibble of its tier, one tier deeper. A sixth tier fails the device with a record; a port above 15 cannot occur, because the hub driver uses a hub's first 15 ports only. One device is enumerated at a time across the whole tree, as across the root ports, since the controller takes one command at a time. The core scans the ports of every running hub on each pass as it scans the root ports, so a device that arrived when the table was full is enumerated once an entry comes free; the refusal is recorded once per port.

The core matches class 9 to the hub driver as it matches class 3 to HID and class 8 to storage: an interface of class 9 in alternate setting 0 with protocol 0, 1 or 2 and an interrupt IN endpoint. `hub.c` reads the hub descriptor, tells the controller through the core's command turn that the slot is a hub with so many ports and, for a high-speed hub, whether it translates per port and its think time, powers every port with its own request whether the descriptor says the ports switch together or one by one, since on a ganged hub one request energises the gang while the specification leaves every other port in its Powered-off state until it has a request of its own (11.11.1), which is what the owner's hub did, and none on a hub that cannot switch, waits the descriptor's power-good time, at least 100 ms since a hub may say 0 and at most the 510 ms its byte can say, and then reads every port's status once, so that a device already plugged in when the hub arrived is found. On a hub with more than 15 ports the driver uses the first 15 and records that. The driver closes, with a record, a hub whose descriptor request fails, whose port power request fails, or that does not answer a request within half a second. A port reset behind a hub is `SET_PORT_FEATURE(PORT_RESET)`, the status read every 20 ms until the reset bit clears with the port enabled, and `C_PORT_RESET` cleared; a reset that has not ended in 500 ms fails the device, and the port is remembered as refused on the hub until its device leaves. The speed comes from the status word's two bits.

For a low or full-speed device below a high-speed hub the core writes into the location the slot of the nearest high-speed hub above it, the port on that hub that leads to the device, and whether that hub was set to translate per port; the same rule holds through a full-speed hub between them. The controller issues the split transactions and this kernel writes none. QEMU's hub is a full-speed hub with no translator, so the translator's words are checked by the host test and proved only by the notebook.

When a hub is pulled, or a hub reports a port's connection gone, everything below goes first: each device's class driver is told, its entry closes through the same closing state as a device on a root port, and the hub's own slot is given back only when nothing below it is still on its way out. A device pulled with a command of its own in flight keeps the command turn until that command answers or the controller gives up on it, so that a slot an Enable Slot answers with after the pull is given back too. A Disable Slot the controller has not answered in five seconds leaves the entry and the slot lost until the next boot, with a record. A connection change is a change of identity: what was on the port is closed before what is there now is looked at. The hub driver reads the change endpoint for as long as the hub is there: one transfer is out whenever no acknowledgement, power request or status read is pending, and after an answer the driver acts on it and puts the next one out. Bit 0 of the answer is the hub's own status, each other bit a port's; the driver reads the status of each one set and clears every change bit with its `CLEAR_PORT_FEATURE`. The hub's own local-power and over-current changes are recorded and cleared. On an enable lost the driver records it and closes the device below; the core enumerates the port again while its connection holds. On an over-current, found by the first scan or reported later, the driver powers the port off and closes the device below, powers the port on again once after a second, and leaves it off with a record when the fault recurs or the power request fails. For a high-speed hub with one translator per port the driver selects the interface's alternate setting 1 first; when the hub stalls that request the driver sets the hub's slot to one translator, and the devices below it are opened with one translator too. A hub whose change endpoint stalls is closed with everything below it. Two hubs are driven at once, each with its refusal bitmap. A SuperSpeed hub is driven by USB 3's rules. It is known by its speed, since the specification puts its protocol 3 in the device descriptor and leaves the interface's at zero: its descriptor is the USB 3 one, it is told its depth in the tree with `SET_HUB_DEPTH` before the controller is told it is a hub, it has no translator and no think time, its ports report SuperSpeed devices and no speed bit, and the three change bits USB 3 adds, the warm reset, the link state and the configuration error, are acknowledged with their own feature numbers. A port whose link has fallen to `SS.Inactive` has the device below it closed and is given a warm reset, `BH_PORT_RESET`; it reports no connection until the reset has ended, and the core then enumerates what is there. A device pulled while its port is being reset gives the enumeration turn back at once, since a port reset is not a controller command and no answer is owed. A USB 3.0 hub is two such devices, this one and a high-speed hub for the USB 2 devices, and on the notebook's blue socket the stick attaches to the SuperSpeed one.

## What is absent, and why

* **Hubs behind hubs on the notebook.** A hub below a hub is driven, two hubs being the driver's table; a third hub is not. A SuperSpeed hub is driven from milestone 43, proved by the host model and the notebook's hub; QEMU has no SuperSpeed hub.
* **Report descriptors.** Above.
* **Isochronous transfers.** Audio and video over USB. Nothing here reads them.
* **Typematic repeat.** A held key on a USB keyboard does not repeat. The PS/2 keyboard's repeat comes from its hardware; a USB host is supposed to make its own, and this one does not.
* **More than one interrupter.** The driver uses one interrupter for every event. When messages are enabled, `isr_alloc_vector` allocates its vector, and `/proc/interrupts` identifies the handler as `xhci`. Several interrupters would need several vectors and a rule for assigning endpoints to them.
* **EHCI.** The companion controller is not driven and will not be. Taking its pins costs one register write. Driving it would cost a controller driver, a hub driver and split transactions, for an interface no machine made after about 2014 has.
* **Booting from a USB disk.** The firmware loads the kernel; this stack starts afterwards.

## The guest's view

`/proc/usb` has one line per configured device: the port, the speed, the identifiers, the class and the protocol. A machine that has had a stick has more: a line per registered USB unit as `diskN VENDOR PRODUCT SECTORS sectors K parts STATE flushes F`, with `disk?` before registration. The sector count is 64-bit. STATE is `reading`, `ready` or `closing`; `ro` follows the flush count for a write-protected unit, and `holds H` follows while closing. The flush count is shared by the units of the device. A last line counts the free USB disk records and the records reclaimed. A machine that never had one writes neither, so a machine with no controller, or one booted with `rach2.nousb`, has the file and it is empty, which is a different answer from the file not being there. A program that counts devices counts the lines that begin with `port`.

The mouse is read through the device Doom opens, by the bare name `mouse`. It opens when a mouse of either kind exists.

`rach2.nousb` switches the stack off, and the probe says `usb: skipped: rach2.nousb` rather than going quiet.

## Stopping

The controller is halted when the machine stops, because other operating systems have a quirk on this exact part for a controller still running as the machine restarts.

It is not given back. Restoring the firmware's interrupt sources and its claim wakes its system-management handler, which re-initialises USB legacy support before this kernel's next instruction runs: on the notebook that took twenty seconds, in the middle of every reboot. The firmware initialises USB again on the next boot whatever this kernel leaves behind. A bring-up that fails is the other case, and it does give the controller back, because a machine with no USB driver should keep whatever its firmware was doing for it.

## Verification

`tests/run host` runs the five host tests written for this stack. `hub_hosttest` drives the core and the hub driver over a model of a tree: the descriptor read and the port count told, the translators of a high-speed hub, the three power modes and the power-good wait, a device found by the scan and reset at its location, a hub pulled with devices below it, the change endpoint and its acknowledgements, over-current, a SuperSpeed hub read through the USB 3 descriptor, told its depth before the controller is told it is a hub, with a device below it opened at SuperSpeed with no translator, USB 3's three change bits acknowledged by their own feature numbers, a link fallen inactive given a warm reset with the device below enumerated again, and a hub that refuses its depth closed; and a device pulled during its reset through the hub giving the turn back. `xhci_hosttest` drives the driver against a model of a controller through `struct xhci_io`: the discovery, the D3 wake, the aperture and register-set bounds, the firmware handoff at once and slowly and never, the halt and reset with its ready wait and its Intel pause, the scratchpad request and its refusal, both context sizes, the rings and their wrap, the command ring and its abort, the ports, the debounce, the device and endpoint commands, and the interrupter: the moderation interval, the two enables, the acknowledgement that keeps the enable beside it, the PCI line taken when messages are refused with its interrupt disable bit cleared and the status register's event bit cleared by the acknowledgement, a device that names no line, the dequeue pointer written with the busy bit so the next event raises an interrupt of its own, and an event written between the acknowledgement and the drain. `usb_hosttest` walks descriptors no device would send, in buffers allocated to the exact length under AddressSanitizer, and drives the core's quiesce over a controller stub: the command turn held across the stop and the dequeue, a halted endpoint reset and one in the Error state only moved on, a refused or unanswered command answered as a failure with nothing retired, and the stick driver told of a slot given back only after Disable Slot's success. `hid_hosttest` hands the class driver reports and reads back what it gave the input layer: a key held, two keys at once, a modifier, an arrow, a rollover, a short report, and an unplug with a key still down. `msc_hosttest` drives the storage driver against a model of a Bulk-Only device that can be told to vanish at any stage: the bring-up in each of its forms, every row of the error matrix, the reads and the writes, the flush and its three answers, the admission queue and its order, a completion delivered between the waiting side's check and its sleep, and a stick pulled out at each stage, which keeps a request whose pages the controller can reach until the slot is given back, the stops before the reset at a timeout, a wrapper over a transfer not yet retired, and completions told apart by generation. `xhci_hosttest` also drives the 32 KiB transfer descriptors: the blocks and their sizes, the Link crossed, the short and the failed block, the retirement and the records held across the next submission, the Stopped events, the endpoint states, the control transfer's stages, and the publication of the blocks.

`tests/run usb` is twenty-one boots against QEMU, listed one by one in [the system tests' Readme](../../../tests/system/Readme.md). Eleven of them take the 8042 away, so that a key typed or a mouse moved on those machines can only have come over USB. What they prove about this stack is that a keyboard and a mouse are enumerated at both device speeds and read through both the shell's buffer and the terminal, that a device plugged in or pulled out after the boot is taken up and taken down, that a device this kernel cannot drive is refused once and costs the others nothing, and that the controller's messages arrive and are counted while the timer's pump stays behind them.

`tests/run subset` boots with `rach2.nousb` and requires the probe to record that it was skipped. Every check added here was proved by a mutation that makes it fail.
