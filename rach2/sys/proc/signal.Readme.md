# Signals

A signal notifies a process or one of its threads of an event: a key the terminal maps to an interrupt, a child that ended, a fault of its own instruction, a timer, or a `kill` from another process. The kernel keeps the signals sent and acts on one each time a thread returns to user mode: it ignores the signal, stops or ends the process, or runs a handler the program installed, on a stack frame laid out as Linux lays it out, so that musl, BusyBox and GNU libc programs built for Linux i386 or x86-64 run unchanged. The code is in `syscall_proc.c`, and the frames are in each architecture's `sigframe.c`. The calls' contracts are in their manual pages, chiefly [sigaction(2)](../../../man/man2/sigaction.2) and [kill(2)](../../../man/man2/kill.2); this document describes how the kernel keeps and delivers signals on both architectures, and [ptrace](ptrace.Readme.md) how a tracer intercepts them. File names are given from `rach2/sys/`, and `kernel.c` is `rach2/kernel.c`.

Signals also reach programs through [wait(2)](../../../man/man2/wait.2), which reports a child's stop, continue and end, `PR_SET_PDEATHSIG` of [prctl(2)](../../../man/man2/prctl.2), [timer_create(2)](../../../man/man2/timer_create.2), and the [core(5)](../../../man/man5/core.5) file a core signal's default action leaves.

Signals 1 to 64 are accepted (`KSIG_MAX` in `lib/ksignal.h`); 1 to 31 have Linux i386's names and numbers, which x86-64 shares. A set is a 64-bit word with bit `signo - 1` for each signal (`KSIG_BIT`), which the calls pass as eight bytes.

### Inside the kernel

`proc/process.h` declares what other subsystems call:

* `signal_post` sends a signal to the process a record belongs to, with a siginfo of `SI_KERNEL`; `signal_post_info` sends one with a siginfo the caller fills; `signal_post_thread` sends one to a single thread, which alone may take it. `signal_post_multi` posts one member's signal of a process-group or all-process send, and keeps it for that process's unfinished forks. `signal_process_target` selects one target per process for these sends: its leader, even when the leader has ended while other threads run. None of these calls posts to a kernel thread (`proc_is_kthread`), as Linux's kernel threads ignore every signal (`kernel/kthread.c:745`).
* `signal_force` sends the signal of a processor exception to the faulting thread. A blocked or ignored signal goes back to its default disposition and is unblocked first, so the process dies unless a handler is installed.
* `signal_set_blocked` is the one way a thread's blocked mask changes under the big lock, and `signal_change_blocked_unlocked` the one way without it.
* `signal_pending` is what an interruptible wait calls before it sleeps and after it wakes; the wait ends with `EINTR` when it returns nonzero.
* `deliver_signals` is called by `isr_leave` (`arch/x86/isr.c`) on each return from an exception, a device interrupt or a system call.
* `proc_child_info` fills the siginfo of a child's `SIGCHLD`, and `tty_signal_group` (`tty/tty.c`) posts a signal to every live user process of a process group.

The posting functions may be called with interrupts off and from an interrupt handler: the PS/2 keyboard posts Ctrl-C's `SIGINT` from its interrupt, and the timer `SIGALRM` from its. They change the 64-bit sets with interrupts off (`sig_irq_save`), since on i686 a read-modify-write of 64 bits is two stores.

## Design

### The state

A process is a thread group (`proc/tgroup.h`), and its signal state is divided as POSIX divides it. The group has the dispositions, `action[1..64]`, each a `struct k_sigaction` (`lib/ksignal.h`) in the layout musl's `rt_sigaction` passes: the handler, or `KSIG_DFL` (0) or `KSIG_IGN` (1), the flags, the restorer and the mask. Each thread's `sigacts` points at that array. The group also has `pending` and `info[1..64]`, the signals sent to the process as a whole and their siginfo; `stop`, the signal of the stop in effect, or 0; and `cpu_left` and `cpu_interval`, the virtual and profiling interval timers.

Each thread's record (`proc/process.h`) has:

* `sig_pending` and `sig_info[1..64]`, the signals sent to that thread alone, and `sig_blocked`, its mask;
* `altstack_sp`, `altstack_size` and `altstack_on`, the alternate stack;
* `saved_sigmask` and `sigmask_saved`, the mask a wait replaced for its duration;
* `restart_kind`, which of Linux's restart codes the handler's EINTR stands for, `restart_due`, set when the dispatcher made that code, and `restart`, the restart block from which `restart_syscall` resumes a sleep, a `poll` or a timed `futex` wait;
* `trap_nr`, `trap_err` and `trap_addr`, the vector, error code and fault address of its last processor exception, which a frame's `sigcontext` reports;
* `stop_seen`, set once the thread has taken the stop in effect;
* in the leader's record, `jc_status`, the stop or continue report for the parent's wait, and `alarm_tick`, `alarm_interval` and `alarm_last`, the real-time interval timer.

A pending signal is one bit, with one `struct k_siginfo` beside it. A signal posted again while it is pending changes nothing, so instances merge, and the siginfo kept is the first one's.

`fork` (`fork_signals` in `syscall_proc.c`) gives the child a group of its own with a copy of the dispositions, the parent's mask, no inherited pending signals, no timers and the parent's alternate stack. A thread made with `CLONE_THREAD` shares its creator's group and takes its mask. A child that shares the address space without `CLONE_VFORK` gets no alternate stack. `execve` (`exec_commit`) puts each caught signal back to its default action, keeps the ignored ones, the mask, the pending signals but those of `SI_TIMER`, and the interval timers, deletes the POSIX timers with their waiting signals, as Linux's `posixtimer_exec` does (`ptimer_exec`), and removes the alternate stack.

### Sending

An ignored signal is dropped before it is queued, whether ignored explicitly or by its default, unless the target thread blocks it, waits for it through `sig_real_blocked`, or is traced and the signal is not `SIGKILL` (`sig_ignored`, Linux 6.12.115, `kernel/signal.c:75-123`). A process-group or all-process send judges one target per process, its leader, zombie or not, selected by `signal_process_target`, as Linux's sends walk the leaders and post to each (`kernel/signal.c:1478-1479, 1635-1639`; `kernel/fork.c:2596-2618`): the leader's credentials decide the permission and its mask whether an ignored signal is kept, and any thread of the process that can takes the signal. Job-control effects still precede this test. Installing an ignored disposition, explicit or default, discards pending instances, as Linux's `do_sigaction` does (`kernel/signal.c:4239-4254`).

`signal_post_info` sets the process's pending bit, keeping the siginfo when the signal was not pending already, applies the job-control rules below, and wakes the one thread `sig_fatal_taker` selects, as Linux's `complete_signal` selects it: the thread the signal was sent to when it can take the signal, and otherwise the first thread that can. The selection uses the pending-work rule below for a handled signal too. When none qualifies, `sig_taker` makes a thread that can take the signal runnable, or the thread it was sent to, without marking new signal work. Every signal set pending, by either call, also wakes the process's signalfd queue (`sig_notify_fd`), as Linux's `__send_signal_locked` calls `signalfd_notify` (`kernel/signal.c:1166-1167`). A process whose threads have all ended takes nothing. `signal_post_thread` sets the thread's own bit and wakes that thread. A thread begins to end, as Linux's `PF_EXITING` marks it, after its exit event in `exit_current` and `terminate_current_process` (`signal_exit_begin`): no signal then picks it, so a signal sent to it in its exit path begins no end of its process, and a signal pending for the process that it could have taken wakes another thread that can, as Linux's `exit_signals` does (`kernel/signal.c:3027-3067`).

A signal fatal to the process begins its end when it is posted, by either call, as Linux 6.12.115's `complete_signal` does (`kernel/signal.c:1034-1056`): the process will end with the signal's wait status, and every thread is sent `SIGKILL` and woken (`sig_complete`). The signal is fatal when its disposition is `SIG_DFL` and its default ends the process without a core dump, a thread would take it now (`sig_fatal_taker`), that thread does not wait for it in `rt_sigtimedwait` (`sig_real_blocked`), the process is not ending already, and the thread it was sent to is not traced, unless the signal is `SIGKILL`. A thread would take it now, as Linux's `wants_signal` judges (`kernel/signal.c:977-993`), when it does not block it and has not begun to end, and either the signal is `SIGKILL`, or the thread is not stopped and is running on a processor or has no unblocked signal of its own and no signal-work flag (`sig_woken`); the thread the signal was sent to is asked first, and for a signal sent to the process every other thread after it. A thread asleep with a signal pending already takes that one first, and its handler may change what the new signal does, so a signal no thread would take now ends nothing at its post. Once a process's end has begun, by `exit_group`, a fatal signal's post or a default action that ends it (`sig_group_end`), its stop in effect and a continue its parent has not collected are cleared, and every signal sent to it is dropped with no effect, but a `SIGKILL` while it writes its core, which stops the core (`sig_prepare`), as Linux's `prepare_signal` drops them (`kernel/signal.c:908-914`). So `SIGTERM`, `SIGHUP` or `SIGINT` at the default action ends a wait that only `SIGKILL` ends: `execve`'s for the other threads, which a sibling held in a wait no signal ends would otherwise prolong, and `vfork`'s for the child. A signal that dumps core is left to the thread that takes it, which writes the core before the other threads end. The `SIGKILL` that `exit_group` and `execve` send the other threads (`sig_post_own`) begins no such end itself, as Linux's `zap_other_threads` sets the bit directly. Any child that `fork`, `vfork` or `clone` makes while an interrupt begins the caller's process's end is sent `SIGKILL` as it is made runnable, with interrupts off (`fork_start`), since the end's `SIGKILL` passes over a child still being built. Waking (`sig_wake`) marks signal work on the chosen thread alone, as Linux's `signal_wake_up_state` sets `TIF_SIGPENDING` (`kernel/signal.c:759-773`); an unchosen sleeping sibling has no such mark merely because a signal is pending for the process. Only the thread itself recomputes the mark, after a mask change or a dequeue, when delivery finds no signal, and before an interruptible wait sleeps again after finding no deliverable signal, as Linux's `recalc_sigpending` does (`kernel/signal.c:157-178`). Waking also makes a thread asleep in the kernel runnable and clears its timed wake; an interruptible wait then calls `signal_pending`, and a wait that goes on arms its deadline again.

An init is protected from signals as Linux 6.12.115 protects one (`SIGNAL_UNKILLABLE`). The group's `unkillable` is set for the system's init when `/sbin/init`, not the debug shell, is the supervisor, and for the first process of each PID namespace below the first (`ns_fork_commit` in `proc/ns.c`). An init under the debug shell, which a probe or a rescue shell runs as, is an ordinary process, since its end returns to the shell. Both posting calls first ask `sig_prepare`, as Linux's `prepare_signal`, whether the signal is set pending at all; an init drops it (`sig_ignored`, `kernel/signal.c:82-123`) when its disposition is the default, unless the thread it is sent to blocks it or waits for it, or is traced and the signal is not `SIGKILL`, or the signal is `SIGKILL` or `SIGSTOP` and forced: sent by the kernel, or by a process in a PID namespace above the init's (`sig_forced`). The system's init drops `SIGKILL` and `SIGSTOP` whoever sends them, unless it is traced and the signal is `SIGSTOP` (`kernel/signal.c:119`). A signal let through begins no init's end at its post unless it is `SIGKILL`, and `deliver_signals` passes over the default action of any but `SIGKILL` and `SIGSTOP` for an init (`kernel/signal.c:2857-2868`). Linux leaves the first of those two tests out, so that a signal one of init's threads blocks ends init through another thread that does not ([the record of faults in other projects](../../../dev-log/upstream.md)). A processor exception's signal (`signal_force`) at its default clears `unkillable` when the init is not traced, so that a faulting init ends rather than fault again.

A signal's job-control effect is applied when it is posted, whatever its disposition and the mask, and before an init drops it (`sig_job_control`), except that a `SIGKILL` an init drops wakes nothing:

* `SIGCONT` discards the pending stop signals, the process's and each thread's. When the process is stopped, it clears the stop, makes runnable every stopped thread that is not in a ptrace stop, records the continue in `jc_status` for `wait4(WCONTINUED)`, and sends the parent `SIGCHLD` with `CLD_CONTINUED`.
* A stop signal, `SIGSTOP`, `SIGTSTP`, `SIGTTIN` or `SIGTTOU`, discards a pending `SIGCONT`.
* `SIGKILL` makes every stopped thread of the process runnable, so that it can take its default action.

`signal_set_blocked` removes `SIGKILL` and `SIGSTOP` from every mask. When the new mask blocks a signal pending for the process that the old mask did not, it wakes another thread that can take the signal, as Linux's `retarget_shared_pending` does, since `sig_taker` may have woken this thread alone for it. Every mask change goes through it: `rt_sigprocmask` when the signal state's lock alone cannot make the change, below, `sigprocmask`, a handler's mask and its return, and the masks of `rt_sigsuspend`, `rt_sigtimedwait`, `ppoll`, `pselect6`, `epoll_pwait` and `epoll_pwait2`. Installing `SIG_IGN` or an ignored default discards a pending instance, the process's and each thread's, and the POSIX timers' notifications of that number that wait (`sig_discard`, `ptimer_discard`); a stop signal discards `SIGCONT` and `SIGCONT` the stops in the same way.

The signal state, every thread's sets, mask and signal-work flag (`sig_woken`), is under the big lock and under `sig_lock`, which stands in for Linux 6.12.115's `sighand->siglock`. A holder of the big lock takes `sig_lock` for each region that reads or changes the sets with interrupts off (`sig_irq_save`), once at the outermost of its nested regions, since a post sends a parent `SIGCHLD` from inside its own. `rt_sigprocmask` runs without the big lock, as Linux's takes only `siglock` (`kernel/signal.c:3097-3136, 3229-3255`): under `sig_lock` alone it reads the mask, makes the new one and marks signal work when the new mask lets a pending signal through (`signal_change_blocked_unlocked`). A mask that stays as it was changes nothing, as Linux's `__set_current_blocked` returns then. Otherwise it takes the big lock and goes through `signal_set_blocked` in two cases: the new mask blocks a signal pending for the process, for which another thread must be woken over the process list, which the big lock covers; or signal work is marked already, since the flag is cleared only by a holder of the big lock, and a flag cleared without it could lose a mark that a stop sets outside `sig_lock` (`sig_group_stop`). The path without the big lock never clears the flag. A post marks the thread it chooses under `sig_lock` after it sets the signal pending, so either the post reads the new mask or the mask's change reads the new signal, and no unblocked signal waits unseen. Only the thread itself and `signal_force`, under `sig_lock`, change a thread's mask; a thread without the big lock reads its own mask under `sig_lock` (`signal_blocked_now`), since the mask is two words on i686. The holders of the big lock take `sig_lock` without the lock-order checker (`raw_spin_lock`), since the big lock orders them and the one holder without it takes nothing under it; the checker would otherwise see it both above and below `timer_lock`, whose holder posts a timer's signal. A thread without the big lock that only tests its own signals, as a wait's `signal_pending` does, takes no lock, as before.

A fork of a new process links a `fork_pending` record on its calling thread's stack into the parent's group before any build step can sleep (`fork_delay_begin`). It then recomputes the caller's signal work and, when any is pending, unlinks the record and returns `RESTART_NOINTR` so that the signal or stop is taken before the call restarts, as `copy_process` does (`kernel/fork.c:2237-2244`). A signal sent to a process group, by `kill` or the terminal, or by `kill(-1)`, reaches these records through `signal_post_multi`, as Linux 6.12.115's `__send_signal_locked` fills `multiprocess` (`kernel/signal.c:1170-1181`). A stop and a continue cancel each other in the saved set. A single-process or thread send adds nothing to it. The new child takes the set when it becomes runnable (`fork_start`), as `copy_process` copies `delayed.signal` (`kernel/fork.c:2605`), with `SI_USER`, pid 0 and uid 0 for a bit without queued siginfo (`kernel/signal.c:598-608`). A failed fork unlinks its record; so does a successful one before a ptrace stop or vfork wait can sleep.

### Permission and senders

`cred_may_signal` decides whether one process may signal another. A privileged sender, one for which `proc_privileged` is true ([the process subsystem's Readme](Readme.md)), may signal any process. Otherwise the sender's real or effective user ID must equal the target's real or saved user ID; the saved ID lets the user who started a set-user-ID program signal it. `SIGCONT` is also allowed within one session: `/bin/su` starts its shell with real user ID 0, and the user's shell that ran `su` as a job resumes it with `SIGCONT`, which the rule of user IDs alone would not allow. Signal 0, which tests a target and sends nothing, has no such exception. A signal from the terminal (`tty_signal_group`) is not checked, since no process sent it.

`kill` (`do_kill`) selects a process by the ID of any of its threads, translated through the caller's PID namespace (`pid_named`), or a process group; `kill(-1)` selects every visible process except the caller's own and its namespace's init, one that has ended and a kernel thread included, and posts only to those the caller may signal (`kill_all`); it succeeds when it selects any process, permitted or not, and fails with `ESRCH` only when it selects none, as Linux's `kill_something_info` counts each process before `group_send_sig_info` checks the permission (`kernel/signal.c:1632-1645`). A process whose threads have all ended takes the signal and discards it until it is reaped, as on Linux. The siginfo is `SI_USER` with the sender's process ID and real user ID translated into the target's namespaces (`sender_info`); `tkill` and `tgkill` post to the thread with `SI_TKILL`. `rt_sigqueueinfo` and `rt_tgsigqueueinfo` post the siginfo the caller gives (`sigframe_take_siginfo`), and refuse a code of 0 or above, or `SI_TKILL`, to another process, since those codes are the kernel's. These calls and `pidfd_send_signal` copy the whole user siginfo before resolving the target, since a file-backed user page fault may sleep while another process reaps the target. The code and permission checks and delivery then run under the big lock without sleeping; `pidfd_send_signal` checks `si_signo` from that same copy.

### Exceptions

A processor exception in user mode belongs to the faulting thread. `_exception_handler` (`arch/x86/isr.c`) and the page fault handler (`mm/paging.c`) record the vector, the error code and, for a page fault, the address in the thread's record, and force the signal with `signal_force`:

| Exception | Signal | `si_code` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Divide error (0) | `SIGFPE` | `FPE_INTDIV` |
| Debug (1) | `SIGTRAP` | `TRAP_HWBKPT` or `TRAP_TRACE`, from DR6 |
| Breakpoint (3), `int3` | `SIGTRAP` | `SI_KERNEL` |
| Invalid opcode (6) | `SIGILL` | `ILL_ILLOPN` |
| Segment not present (11), stack fault (12) | `SIGBUS` | `SI_KERNEL` |
| Page fault (14) | `SIGSEGV` | `SEGV_MAPERR` outside every region, `SEGV_ACCERR` inside one |
| Page fault for which no frame could be had | `SIGKILL` | `SI_KERNEL` |
| x87 error (16), SIMD error (19) | `SIGFPE` | the first of `FPE_FLTINV`, `FPE_FLTDIV`, `FPE_FLTOVF`, `FPE_FLTUND` and `FPE_FLTRES` the unit raised unmasked |
| Alignment check (17) | `SIGBUS` | `BUS_ADRALN` |
| Any other, the protection fault (13) and the overflow of `into` (4) among them | `SIGSEGV` | `SI_KERNEL` |

The divide error, the invalid opcode, the floating-point errors and the debug exception give the saved instruction pointer as `si_addr`, and the page fault the address it touched. A handler that returns without changing the saved registers runs a faulting instruction again; after a trap, `int3`, `into` or a single step, the program goes on with the next instruction, since the processor saves that instruction's address for a trap. The out-of-memory `SIGKILL` is forced because a `SIGSEGV` handler that returned would fault again. The system's init is ended directly instead, as [memory](../mm/Readme.md#page-faults) describes, since it drops even a forced `SIGKILL`. A seccomp filter's `SECCOMP_RET_TRAP` forces `SIGSYS` the same way (`proc/seccomp.c`). A debug exception in kernel mode is not the program's fault; [ptrace](ptrace.Readme.md) describes it.

### Waits

`signal_pending` returns nonzero when the thread has a signal pending, its own or its process's, that it does not block and does not ignore: one with a handler, or one whose default action ends or stops the process. A signal ignored by `SIG_IGN` or by a default of ignoring ends no wait. It also returns nonzero when a tracer requested a stop with `PTRACE_INTERRUPT` or a group stop has not yet been taken. Before returning zero it takes each ignored signal it passed over, the thread's and the process's, as a delivery takes it (`sig_dequeue`), and drops it, as `get_signal` dequeues each ignored signal and then drops it (`kernel/signal.c:2821, 2838-2839, 2853-2854`): a POSIX timer's signal has its timer's overrun counted and the timers' notifications that waited for it posted (`ptimer_rearm`), as `dequeue_signal` rearms the timer (`kernel/signal.c:686-699`). It then recomputes `sig_woken` with interrupts off, so neither an ignored signal nor a signal another thread took leaves a stale mark on the sleeper.

The interruptible waits call it: `wq_wait_event` (`proc/wait_queue.h`), which pipes, sockets and terminals sleep in, `poll_wait` (`io/poll.c`), the sleeps, the wait for a child (`wait_find`), and the waits for a signal. `wq_wait_event` tests its condition first, so a wait whose event has happened returns its result with a signal pending, and a call that moved bytes before the signal returns their count. `wq_wait_event_nointr`, for a lock another process has or a transfer a device has in flight, does not call it. Each call's page gives its own rule.

`pause` and `rt_sigsuspend` wait in `sig_wait` until `signal_pending` returns nonzero, and return `EINTR`; the signal acts on the way out. `rt_sigtimedwait` takes a signal of its set instead (`sigwait_take`). While it waits the set is unblocked, so that `sig_taker` can select the waiting thread, and the old mask comes back when it returns; it is kept meanwhile in `sig_real_blocked`, as Linux's `do_sigtimedwait` keeps it in `real_blocked` (`kernel/signal.c:3687-3697`), so that a signal of the set the waiter takes does not end the process at its post. A signalfd read leaves the mask as it is, as Linux's `signalfd_dequeue` does (`fs/signalfd.c:154-194`): a signal of its mask that another thread does not block goes to that thread, or ends the process, and the read takes only what no thread takes. It sleeps on the process's signalfd queue (`tg->sigfd`, Linux's `signalfd_wqh`), which every signal sent to the process or to one of its threads wakes (`sig_notify_fd`), and `poll` finds a signalfd readable while a signal of its set is pending for the poller and waits on the same queue.

### Delivery

`isr_leave` calls `deliver_signals` with interrupts off and the big lock held; the entries that take no lock, the interprocessor interrupt's among them, return without it ([the architectures' Readme](../arch/Readme.md)). On a return to kernel mode it does nothing. On a return to user mode it frees the records of threads that have ended (`reap_threads`), makes the stops a tracer requested of a traced thread (`ptrace_return_stops`), and then loops:

1. When the process is stopped and the thread has not taken the stop, the thread takes it (`group_stop_taken`): it parks in `sig_park` until a `SIGCONT` or a `SIGKILL`, or, when traced, makes a ptrace stop instead.
2. A pending `SIGKILL`, the thread's or its process's, is taken off both sets and ends the process (`terminate_current_process`), as Linux's `get_signal` ends a thread of a process whose end has begun (`kernel/signal.c:2772-2784`), even when a lower-numbered signal with a handler is pending beside it. With it off, the thread's exit event stops for a tracer that asked for it. Otherwise it takes one signal it does not block off the pending sets (`sig_dequeue`): the lowest-numbered of its own, or when it has none, the lowest-numbered of its process's, as Linux's `dequeue_signal` takes them. With none, the loop ends.
3. A traced thread stops for its tracer, which may replace the signal or cancel it (`ptrace_signal_stop`).
4. An ignored signal is dropped. A default action is the one `ksig_default_action` gives, which [sigaction(2)](../../../man/man2/sigaction.2) lists: to ignore; to stop the process (`sig_group_stop`), after which the loop starts again at step 1; or to end it, with a core file for the core signals (`terminate_current_process`).
5. A caught signal gets a frame (`sigframe_setup`), and `deliver_signals` returns. One handler is entered on each return to user mode; another pending signal is taken at the next return, which may come during the handler.

For a handler, `deliver_signals` adds the action's mask to the thread's, and the signal itself unless `SA_NODEFER` is set, and with `SA_RESETHAND` puts the disposition back to the default for the next signal. The frame records the mask to restore: the thread's own, or the one a wait replaced. `SA_NOCLDSTOP` and `SA_NOCLDWAIT` are stored and do nothing; the other flags act as the sections below describe.

A stop (`sig_group_stop`) sets the group's `stop`, records `(signo << 8) | 0x7f` in the leader's `jc_status` for `wait4(WUNTRACED)`, and sends the parent `SIGCHLD` with `CLD_STOPPED`. It marks signal work on every other live thread and wakes it, so each leaves an interruptible wait and parks at its next return to user mode, as Linux 6.12.115's `do_signal_stop` does (`kernel/signal.c:2499-2511`). `jc_status` is one slot, so a stop and a continue the parent has not yet collected replace each other's report. A stop aimed at init is dropped, since a stopped init would leave orphans with no reaper, unless the debug shell is the supervisor, as in a rescue boot and on the test image.

A default that ends the process calls `terminate_current_process` (`kernel.c`), which runs the teardown as a system call runs, with interrupts on and `in_syscall` set, since the core file and the release of the files may sleep on a disk that answers only by interrupt. It sends the other threads `SIGKILL` (`proc_group_exit`), writes the core file ([the process subsystem's Readme](Readme.md) describes how), and the last thread to end publishes the process's end with the signal in its wait status. When the process's end has begun already, by `exit_group` or a fatal signal's post, nothing is sent, no core is written and the process keeps the status that began its end; that test runs with interrupts off, since a signal posted from an interrupt may begin the end too. An init that a signal ends panics the kernel (`panic_init_died`), unless the debug shell is the supervisor.

### Restarting a system call

A system call that a signal cuts short returns `-EINTR` and says in `restart_kind` how Linux would restart it, by the code Linux's call returns: `RESTART_SYS` (`ERESTARTSYS`), which the dispatcher sets before each call, restarts after no handler and after a handler with `SA_RESTART`; `RESTART_NOINTR` (`ERESTARTNOINTR`, the `futex` waits for a PI lock and a fork with signal work pending at entry) restarts always; `RESTART_NOHAND` (`ERESTARTNOHAND`: `pause`, the `sigsuspend`s, `ppoll`, `select`, `pselect6`, an absolute `clock_nanosleep`, `msgsnd` and `msgrcv`) restarts only when no handler runs; and `RESTART_BLOCK` (`ERESTART_RESTARTBLOCK`: `nanosleep`, a relative `clock_nanosleep`, `poll` and a timed `futex` wait) restarts only when no handler runs, through `restart_syscall`. The dispatcher (`_syscall_handler` in `syscall.c`) translates EINTR into that code in the result register, unless it is one that Linux never restarts (`syscall_restartable`), whose `EINTR` reaches the caller in every case: `rt_sigtimedwait`, `epoll_wait`, `epoll_pwait`, `epoll_pwait2`, `semop` and `semtimedop`, directly or through i686's `ipc` (`sysv_ipc_restartable`), an `ioctl` of KVM's, type 0xAE (`ioctl_restartable`), `restart_syscall` with nothing to resume, and the two returns from a handler, whose result register is the interrupted call's. The test compares handlers, not numbers, so one list serves both architectures, and `ia32_never_restarts` judges an i386 call's compat handler. `deliver_signals` reads the live result register and `orig_nr` after signal selection and the tracer's stops (`signal_restart` in `syscall_proc.c`). With a call number other than -1, it restarts when the result code lets the handler that runs, or the lack of one, restart it, as after a stop and a continue or a signal that is ignored: it puts the call's number back in EAX or RAX, or for `RESTART_BLOCK` the number of `restart_syscall`, 0 on i386 and 219 on x86-64, and moves the instruction pointer back over `int $0x80` or `syscall`, two bytes either way (`SYSCALL_INSN_LEN`). Otherwise a restart code becomes `EINTR`. The dispatcher marks a call it translated (`restart_due`, cleared at the next entry), and `deliver_signals` counts the mark as signal work, as Linux keeps `TIF_SIGPENDING` set until `get_signal` runs and restarts the call when `get_signal` finds nothing: a wait that saw a process-directed signal another thread then took, or that slept after its signal check, as on a fault copying `poll`'s results out, restarts and never returns a raw restart code. With `orig_nr` -1, or with no pending signal or stop to take and no translated call, the result stays unchanged; only a code a tracer wrote into a call that was not interrupted reaches the program, as on Linux.

A call restarted through `restart_syscall` resumes from its block (`struct restart_block`), which it filed when the signal cut it short: the sleep's end by the tick and the clock, so that the restarted sleep ends at the first deadline, the user's remainder pointer and its width, `poll`'s descriptors and deadline, or the `futex` word, value, bitset and deadline tick. A direct `restart_syscall` with no block returns `EINTR`, as Linux's `do_no_restart_syscall` does; the return from a handler clears the block (`signal_return`), and each sleep clears it as it begins. A tracer reads the restart code at both the call's exit stop and the signal-delivery stop and can change or cancel the restart ([ptrace](ptrace.Readme.md)).

`rt_sigsuspend`, and `ppoll`, `pselect6`, `epoll_pwait` and `epoll_pwait2` through `poll_mask_set` (`io/poll.c`), install a mask for their wait and keep the old one in `saved_sigmask`. When a signal ends the wait, the handler's frame records the old mask, so the handler runs under the wait's mask and its return restores the old one; when no handler runs, `deliver_signals` puts the old mask back at once. A wait that ends for another reason puts it back itself.

### The frames

`sigframe_setup` in each architecture's `sigframe.c` builds the frame Linux builds, below the user stack pointer, or at the top of the alternate stack for an `SA_ONSTACK` action when one is set and the thread is not on it already. The floating-point state goes above the frame, placed and written by `fpu_frame_place` and `fpu_frame_put` (`arch/x86/fpu.c`) from the thread's own area, which `fpu_save` fills first; [sigaction(2)](../../../man/man2/sigaction.2) gives its layout with and without XSAVE. A frame that cannot be written, or that would run past the bottom of the alternate stack, ends the process with `SIGSEGV`. A new program's auxiliary vector gives `AT_MINSIGSTKSZ`, the most bytes a frame takes (`sigframe_stack_bytes`).

| | i686 | x86-64 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Frame of a handler without `SA_SIGINFO` | `struct sigframe` | `struct rt_sigframe` |
| Frame of a handler with `SA_SIGINFO` | `struct rt_sigframe` | `struct rt_sigframe` |
| The handler returns to | `sa_restorer` with `SA_RESTORER`, else the frame's `retcode` | `sa_restorer`; without `SA_RESTORER` the process ends with `SIGSEGV`, as on Linux |
| Left free below the stack pointer | nothing | the 128-byte red zone |
| Alignment at the handler's entry | `ESP + 4` a multiple of 16 | `RSP + 8` a multiple of 16 |
| Signal number, siginfo, ucontext | EAX, and for an rt frame EDX and ECX | RDI, RSI, RDX, with RAX 0 |

On i686 (`arch/i686/sigframe.c`), `struct sigframe` has the return address, the signal number, the `struct sigcontext`, an unused floating-point area of 624 bytes, kept so that the next field is where old programs read it, `extramask`, signals 33 to 64 of the mask to restore, and an 8-byte `retcode`, `popl %eax; movl $119, %eax; int $0x80`. `struct rt_sigframe` has the return address, the signal number, the addresses of the siginfo and the ucontext, the 128-byte `siginfo_t`, the `ucontext_t` (flags, link, alternate stack, `sigcontext` and the 8-byte mask), and an 8-byte `retcode`, `movl $173, %eax; int $0x80`. Debuggers recognize a signal frame by these two return codes.

On x86-64 (`arch/x86_64/sigframe.c`), `struct rt_sigframe` has the return address, then the `ucontext_t` (flags, link, alternate stack, the 256-byte `sigcontext` and the mask), then the 128-byte `siginfo_t`. The ucontext's flags have `UC_SIGCONTEXT_SS` and `UC_STRICT_RESTORE_SS`, as Linux sets them.

On x86-64, an action installed by an i386 call, `signal`, `sigaction` or `rt_sigaction` made by `int $0x80`, is marked `SA_IA32_ABI` (`signal_action`), as Linux marks it, and no call reports the mark. Its handler gets i386's frames from `ia32_sigframe_setup` (`arch/x86_64/sigframe32.c`), laid out as on i686 and run in 32-bit code with CS 0x23 and SS 0x2B, with the i386 siginfo (`ia32_fill_siginfo`) and the floating-point state as `fpu_frame32_put` builds it: the 112-byte `fnsave` header, converted from the `fxsave64` image as Linux converts it, before the image. For a non-flat SS, an action without SA_ONSTACK or SA_RESTORER uses a nonzero `sa_restorer` as its stack top unless already on the alternate stack, and checks that the new frame fits that stack, as Linux does. The frame returns to `sa_restorer` under SA_RESTORER, else the i386 vDSO's return (`ia32_vdso_sigreturn`), else its own `retcode`. A `sigreturn` or `rt_sigreturn` made by `int $0x80` restores an i386 frame (`ia32_sigframe_restore`). The calls that pass a siginfo read and write it in the caller's layout (`siginfo_to_caller`, `siginfo_from_caller`).

On both, the `sigcontext` has the interrupted registers and selectors, the trap number, error code and fault address from the thread's record, the floating-point state's address and the mask's first word; the ucontext's flags have `UC_FP_XSTATE` when the state is an XSAVE image; the frame's alternate stack has `SS_ONSTACK` only when the signal came while the thread was on that stack; and the handler starts with the direction, trap and resume flags clear and the initial floating-point state (`fpu_reset`).

The siginfo (`sigframe_fill_siginfo`) has the signal, `si_errno` and the code, then the fields of its kind: the address for `SIGSEGV`, `SIGBUS`, `SIGILL`, `SIGFPE` and `SIGTRAP` with a code above 0; the call's address, number and architecture for a seccomp `SIGSYS`; and otherwise the sender's or child's process ID and user ID, and a child's status or the value of a timer or a queued signal. On i686 those fields begin at byte 12; on x86-64 they begin at byte 16, after the padding that aligns the union, and a value is eight bytes.

### The return from a handler

The restorer makes `sigreturn` for a plain frame on i686, and `rt_sigreturn` for an rt frame. `signal_return` (`syscall_proc.c`) calls `sigframe_restore`, sets the mask it read through `signal_set_blocked`, and returns the restored result register, so that the dispatcher's store of the result leaves it as it was. `signal_return` also sets `orig_nr` to -1, so another pending signal leaves the restored result alone. The frame is found from the stack pointer, which the handler's `ret` moved past the return address: on i686 the frame begins 4 bytes below it for `rt_sigreturn`, and 8 bytes below for `sigreturn`, whose restorer also pops the signal number; on x86-64 it begins 8 bytes below.

`sigframe_restore` takes back the general registers, the instruction and stack pointers, and of the flags only those a program may change, the arithmetic flags and the direction, trap, resume and alignment-check flags (`FIX_EFLAGS`). It loads the floating-point state through `fpu_frame_load`, which reads it into an area from the heap and loads it from there, since the copy from the user stack may sleep for a page and the switch away would save the registers over the thread's own area; a frame whose state address is 0 gets the initial state. From an rt frame it also takes back the alternate stack, as `sigaltstack` would take it, unless the thread is on that stack or the size is under 2048 bytes.

The code and stack selectors are always the user's. On i686 a data selector stays only when it is the user data selector, 0x23, or the TLS selector, 0x33, and becomes 0x23 otherwise, since the return loads it in ring 0, where any other would fault the kernel ([thread-local storage](../arch/tls.Readme.md)). On x86-64 an instruction pointer outside the lower half is refused, since `iretq` would fault on it. A frame that cannot be read, a state that `xrstor` or `fxrstor` would fault on, or a return for which the heap has no area ends the process with `SIGSEGV`; `fpu_frame_take` (`arch/x86/fpu.c`) makes the checks, and [sigaction(2)](../../../man/man2/sigaction.2) lists them.

### What the kernel sends

* `SIGCHLD` to the parent at a child's end (`proc_exit_cleanup` in `proc/exit.c`), with `proc_child_info`'s siginfo: `CLD_EXITED` and the exit code, or `CLD_KILLED` or `CLD_DUMPED` and the signal, with the child's process ID and real user ID translated into the parent's namespaces; at its stop, with `CLD_STOPPED`; and at its continue, with `CLD_CONTINUED`. Init, or a child subreaper, gets one when it adopts a child that has already ended (`reparent_children` in `proc/process.c`), and a tracer one with `CLD_TRAPPED` for each ptrace stop.
* The signal a process set with `PR_SET_PDEATHSIG`, when its parent ends, once its new parent has adopted it (`reparent_children`).
* `SIGALRM` from the timer tick when the leader's real-time timer is due (`proc_wake_sleepers`), and `SIGVTALRM` and `SIGPROF` when the process's processor-time timers run out (`sched_account_one` in `proc/sched.c`), each to the process; [setitimer(2)](../../../man/man2/setitimer.2) gives the rules.
* A POSIX timer's signal with `SI_TIMER` ([the I/O Readme](../io/Readme.md)), and a message queue's notification with `SI_MESGQ` (`ipc/mqueue.c`).
* `SIGPIPE` to the writing thread, with `SI_USER` from its own process, for a write to a pipe or FIFO with no reader (`ipc/pipe.c`) and a send on an `AF_UNIX` socket whose peer is gone, unless the flags have `MSG_NOSIGNAL` (`ipc/unix.c`). A TCP send posts none ([send(2)](../../../man/man2/send.2)).
* `SIGXFSZ` to the writing thread for a write or a truncation past its `RLIMIT_FSIZE` (`fsize_refused` in `syscall_fs.c`).
* `SIGINT`, `SIGQUIT` and `SIGTSTP` to the foreground process group for the terminal's interrupt, quit and suspend characters, `SIGHUP` when a pseudo-terminal's master closes, and `SIGWINCH` when its window size is set ([the terminal Readme](../tty/Readme.md)).
* `SIGTTIN` to the process group of a background process that reads its controlling terminal, and `SIGTTOU` to that of one that writes it while `TOSTOP` is set or changes it with `TCSETS` and its kin, `TCFLSH`, `TCXONC` or `TIOCSPGRP`, as Linux's `tty_check_change`. The call returns `EINTR` and is restarted, as above, once the group is continued. A caller that blocks or ignores `SIGTTIN` fails its read with `EIO`, and one that blocks or ignores `SIGTTOU` goes ahead; a caller in an orphaned process group, which no member's parent in the session can continue, fails with `EIO` where it would otherwise take the signal (`tty_job_check` in `tty/tty.c`).
* `SIGKILL` to the other threads of a process that `exit_group` or a fatal signal ends (`proc_group_exit`), to every thread of a process a fatal signal is posted to (`sig_complete`), and to the other threads of a process one of whose threads calls `execve` (`exec_one_thread`), and to every process of a PID namespace whose init ends (`proc/ns.c`).
* `SIGSYS` for a call a seccomp filter traps; `SIGTRAP` at the `execve` of a thread a tracer attached without `PTRACE_SEIZE` and without the exec option; and `SIGSTOP` to a thread `PTRACE_ATTACH` attaches.

## Limits

* A signal has one pending instance in each set. One sent again while it is pending merges with it and its siginfo is lost; real-time signals are not queued, so values `sigqueue` sends faster than they are taken are lost.
* `SA_NOCLDSTOP` and `SA_NOCLDWAIT` have no effect, and ignoring `SIGCHLD` reaps nothing: a parent always collects its children's ends with a wait.
* `SS_AUTODISARM` is accepted and has no effect.
* i686 registers none of i386's older `signal`, `sigaction`, `sigsuspend` and `sigpending` calls, and no `rt_sigtimedwait_time64`; each answers `ENOSYS`. x86-64 has no `sigreturn` or `sigprocmask`, as on Linux.

## Tests

`tests/run default` runs every guest probe, each in a boot of its own on the test image ([the tests' Readme](../../../tests/Readme.md)). Among them:

* `tests/sigprobe.c`: a handler and its return through `rt_sigreturn`, a blocked signal delivered once unblocked, a default termination, a handler that writes invalid selectors into its own frame, `SA_RESETHAND`, a sleep a signal ends where it lands (judged by `sleeps_interrupted` in `/proc/stat`, not by time), and the real-time interval timer.
* `tests/trapprobe.c`: an invalid opcode and a divide error end only the faulting child, with `SIGILL` and `SIGFPE`.
* `tests/ownprobe.c`: the permission rule, the saved user ID, the session's `SIGCONT`, and the stricter signal 0.
* `tests/waitprobe.c` and `tests/pipeprobe.c`: a socket receive a signal interrupts, and `SIGPIPE` with `EPIPE`.
* `tests/jobprobe.c`: group signals, a stop, a continue and a kill, and the background read's `SIGTTIN`.
* `tests/xsaveprobe.c`: the frame's XSAVE image, a handler's change to it, and a damaged XSAVE header, which must end the process with `SIGSEGV`.
* `tests/musl/sigmusl.c`: both restorers through musl, the mask, `abort`, and the context an `SA_SIGINFO` handler receives.
* `tests/musl/sigframemusl.c`: a fault handler that returns to its instruction, `SEGV_MAPERR`, `SA_RESTART` and its absence, `SA_ONSTACK`, `int3`, `rt_sigpending`, the old `sigprocmask`, `sigsuspend`'s mask, `SIGCHLD`'s siginfo, and a nested handler that runs off a small alternate stack.
* `tests/musl/sigwakemusl.c`: a thread woken in a pipe read by a `SIGCHLD` its leader blocks and it ignores, which discards it and sleeps again, so that a `SIGTERM` the leader blocks still ends the process through that thread; and `kill(-1, 0)` from an unprivileged process, which returns 0 though it may signal none of the processes it selects.
* `tests/musl/threadmusl.c`: `pthread_kill`, a signal to the process that waits while every thread blocks it, and a second signal another thread takes while the first one's handler runs.
* `tests/musl/callsmusl.c`: `sigtimedwait`, `sigqueue`, `rt_sigqueueinfo`, a POSIX timer's overrun, and a signalfd.
* `tests/musl/waitidmusl.c`, the siginfo of a child's exit, death, stop and continue; `tests/musl/prctlmusl.c`, the parent-death signal; `tests/musl/seccompmusl.c`, `SIGSYS`; and `tests/musl/pwauthmusl.c`, signals above 34 and musl's refusal of 32 to 34.

Other suites, each with a line in [the suite inventory](../../../tests/system/suites.md):

* `tests/run fault` requires `killed by signal 11` from three probes that touch memory they may not.
* `tests/run conload` requires the interrupt character typed at the console to reach a foreground child under load, also behind 200 lines no one has read.
