# ext2

The ext2 file system is the type `ext2` of `mount(2)` and the root of the installed system, read and written through the buffer cache (`bcache.h`). It exists so that the root on a disk has a standard format with a standard checker: the host's `mke2fs` makes the volumes (`tools/image/mkext2.sh`), and [e2fsck(8)](../../../man/man8/e2fsck.8) repairs them. The driver repairs nothing but free counts. It checks a volume not unmounted cleanly before writing to it, and on a `sync` or `dirsync` mount writes in an order that leaves every stop repairable without losing a file's data. The code is the same on i686 and x86-64. Its code is in six files, which share `ext2_internal.h`: `ext2.c` has the mount, the superblock, the inode table and the nodes in memory; `ext2_alloc.c` the bitmaps, the group descriptors and the free counts; `ext2_batch.c` the file-write batch; `ext2_dir.c` the directories and the operations on names; `ext2_file.c` a file's data, its block map and its `fsync`; and `ext2_check.c` the check at a writable mount. `ext2xattr.c` is the codec of the attribute block.

## Interfaces

`ext2.h` declares one record, `vfs_ext2_type`: the name `ext2`, the constructor `ext2_mount`, the operations, `file_locked` and `name_cache` set, and `ns_serialised` and `nodev` clear (`vfs.h`). `mount(2)` finds it by name, and the boot path mounts an ext2 root with it through `vfs_init_root`, on the partition the boot line selects as [rach2/Readme.md](../../Readme.md) says. Of the VFS operations it leaves out `lookup_rest`, `pin` and `unpin`. The constructor and a remount take one option, `errors=continue`, `errors=remount-ro` or `errors=panic`, which overrides the superblock's `s_errors` as Linux's `errors=` does, and ignore the others; another value of `errors=` is `EINVAL`, logged as `ext2: refused: errors=`. The driver reads three mount flags: `MS_RDONLY`, and `MS_SYNCHRONOUS` and `MS_DIRSYNC`, either of which makes the mount strict.

What a program sees is in the pages of the calls: [mount(2)](../../../man/man2/mount.2) for `sync`, `dirsync` and remount, [statfs(2)](../../../man/man2/statfs.2), [getdents64(2)](../../../man/man2/getdents64.2), [mknod(2)](../../../man/man2/mknod.2), [symlink(2)](../../../man/man2/symlink.2), [ioctl(2)](../../../man/man2/ioctl.2) for the inode flags, and [sync(2)](../../../man/man2/sync.2) for `fsync` and its recorded error. The kernel log has each mount as `ext2: mounted: N blocks of B, I inodes, G groups`, with `, read-only` for a read-only mount, and the records named below.

## Design

### The format

The driver takes revision 0 and 1 superblocks, blocks of 1024, 2048 and 4096 bytes, and inodes of 128 and 256 bytes. It reads the primary superblock at byte 1024 and the primary descriptor table at block `s_first_data_block + 1`, and never reads or writes the backup copies.

* Incompatible features: `filetype` is the one known; any other fails the mount, which excludes ext4's extents, 64-bit numbers and flexible groups, and a journal that needs recovery.
* Read-only-compatible features: `sparse_super`, `large_file` and `extra_isize` are known; any other mounts the volume read-only, and so does an `extra_isize` volume whose minimum extra size (`s_min_extra_isize`) is not a multiple of 4 or passes the inode.
* Compatible features are ignored, as Linux 6.12.115's ext2 ignores them (`fs/ext2/ext2.h:545`, `fs/ext2/super.c:933-946`), `has_journal` among them, so a journal is neither replayed nor written, except that the descriptor blocks `resize_inode` reserves must fit the volume.
* A block size below 4096 mounts the volume read-only: `bcache_write` writes a whole 4 KiB buffer, and two dependent metadata blocks in one buffer could not be written in order.

`tools/image/mkext2.sh` formats with 4 KiB blocks, 256-byte inodes, `sparse_super`, `large_file`, `extra_isize`, `filetype` and `resize_inode`, and gives every node the date of the checkout's commit as its four times. Images made before it have 128-byte inodes and no `extra_isize`, and mount as before.

The constructor fails with `EINVAL`, logged as `ext2: refused: WHAT`, when the magic is not `0xEF53` or the revision or sizes are not those above; the first data block is not 1 at 1 KiB blocks and 0 otherwise; a count is zero, out of range, or more than a bitmap block, the device or the groups hold; the descriptor table does not fit; a group's bitmaps or inode table lie outside it or overlap, or its free count passes its size; or the root inode is not a directory. A volume the driver mounts but does not write is logged as `ext2: mounted read-only: WHY`.

### Reading

An inode is the first 128 bytes of its slot and, in a 256-byte slot, the extra fields after them as far as `i_extra_isize` at byte 128 says: the extra words of the change, modification and access times at bytes 132, 136 and 140, and the creation time and its extra word at 144 and 148. An extra word has the nanoseconds in its high 30 bits and in its low two bits 32 and 33 of the seconds, added to the signed 32 bits of the low word, as Linux's ext4 has them. An `i_extra_isize` that is not a multiple of 4 or passes the slot makes the inode `EIO`, logged as `ext2: inode N: extra size X does not fit its 256 bytes`. The bytes past the creation time, in-inode extended attributes among them, are neither read nor written. The owner and group have their high 16 bits at bytes 120 and 122, and a regular file's size its high word at byte 108, so a regular file's size is 64 bits and a directory's 32.

Every number from the disk is checked before use. An inode number must be within the count, and an inode's `i_extra_isize` a multiple of 4 inside its slot. A block pointer must lie inside the volume and outside the structural blocks: the boot block, superblock copies, descriptor tables, bitmaps and inode tables (`ext2_data_block_ok`). A directory entry must lie inside its block and the directory's size, with a `rec_len` of at least 8 and a multiple of 4, its name inside the record and its inode within the count. A fault is `EIO` and a log record such as `ext2: inode N: block B is out of range`; a bad pointer, entry or inode met at run time is also reported as Linux's `ext2_error` reports it (`ext2_mark_error`), each time it is met, while the check of a writable mount or remount judges the volume itself and reports nothing (`ext2_inode_check_load`). The error bit is set in memory and, on a writable mount, the superblock written at once under `em->super`, so that the next writable mount checks the volume. Then the mount's policy applies, from `s_errors`, whose unknown values count as `remount-ro` as in Linux, or from the `errors=` option: `continue` goes on; `remount-ro` makes a writable mount read-only at once, logged as `ext2: error: remounting read-only`: the driver stops writing in the same hold of `em->super`, so that no later step of an operation under way changes the volume and `ext2_write_owned` refuses it with `EROFS`, the batch's writes accepted before are drained by the flusher, a sync or the next remount writable, and only that remount, which checks the volume first, makes the mount write again; and `panic` stops the kernel with `ext2: panic from previous error`. `mke2fs` writes `continue`. A read-only mount sets the bit in memory alone, which a remount writable puts on the disk after its check.

An inode in use is a node in memory (`struct ext2_node`), from the heap, held by whatever uses it and freed at its last `put`; the name cache holds one for each name it keeps, thousands of them. Its count of holds changes in one atomic step (`ext2_node_ref`), since the cached walk takes a hold on a node the name cache holds and gives back one that is not the last without the big lock (`ext2_put_unless_last`, [vfs.Readme.md](vfs.Readme.md)); the count reaches 0, and an unlinked inode's put drains the batch, only under the big lock. A mount keeps its nodes on a list, for an unmount, and in a hash by inode number, for `ext2_node_for`: the number's low bits pick the bucket, so consecutive inodes have buckets of their own. The hash starts with 64 buckets in the mount's record and doubles from the heap whenever the nodes outnumber its buckets; a heap with no room leaves it as it is. `ext2_node_visits` in `/proc/stat` counts the nodes the searches read, [proc(5)](../../../man/man5/proc.5).

A node has a state. It enters the hash `N_LOADING`, before its inode is read or, for a new inode, in the step that picks the number, and goes `N_LIVE` once read or written. A second caller on a loading node takes a hold and sleeps on `node_wq`, so that one inode has one node; a load that fails makes it `N_FAILED`, wakes them with the failure, and the last hold frees it. References change between sleeps, with no lock. The last put of a linked inode frees the node in that step. The last put of an unlinked inode makes it `N_DYING`, which takes no new hold: a caller that finds one waits for it to go and looks again, which only a damaged volume makes anyone do. A caller with no file lock frees the inode there; any other leaves it on `em->reap`, since the free takes node locks out of order and waits on the disk, and it is done at the end of the operation, at the last `unlock_node` or `put`, or in `sync`, which every unmount passes. `ext2_inode_alloc` passes over a number in the hash, whose bit may be clear while it dies.

A file is read through 12 direct pointers and single, double and triple indirect blocks; a hole reads as zeros. A read does not change the access time.

A regular file's data is read into the file page cache past the buffer cache (`read_frames`, [vfs.Readme.md](vfs.Readme.md#types-and-operations)), as Linux's `mpage_readahead` reads a file's pages into the page cache's folios (fs/mpage.c): read(2)'s readahead asks for a window of up to what one request to the device moves, and a fault's read-around for up to 32 pages ([the memory Readme](../mm/Readme.md#the-file-page-cache)); a call plans 32 pages on its stack and a longer one from the heap, and the pages whose blocks follow each other on the device go in one transfer into the cache's frames themselves (`block_read_frames`, [the block Readme](../block/Readme.md#the-block-request)), so that no buffer holds the bytes a second time and no copy moves them. Data written to a file goes through the batch and the buffer cache, so the device's bytes of a data block that neither holds are the file's; a page whose block the batch has an image of or the buffer cache holds, clean or dirty, is read as `read` reads it, from them, as Linux's `do_mpage_readpage` falls back to the buffers of a page that has them, and so is a page of blocks smaller than a page that are not one run on the device, which `do_mpage_readpage` gives to `block_read_full_folio`. The node's lock is held from the look at the blocks to the end of their transfer, so that no write, truncation or free of the file changes a block in between, as Linux's locked folio holds off a truncation until its read ends; the big lock only across the look, while the batch is not empty. Readahead gives a completion, and then each run of sectors is only started under the lock (`block_read_frames_async`), the completion called for each page from the device's answer, or in the call for a hole and a copied page; such a run takes the file's last page as the device has it, whose bytes past the size are zeros there, since a truncation clears them and a new block's image starts as zeros, as Linux's `do_mpage_readpage` reads the last block whole; a free of the file's blocks (`ext2_punch`) then waits for the reads started over them that are still in flight (`block_settle`) before it gives the blocks back. Metadata, directories and symbolic links are read through the buffer cache as before.

The type sets `reads_unlocked` ([vfs.Readme.md](vfs.Readme.md#types-and-operations)), so `read`, `readdir`, `readlink`, `getattr`, `open`, `release`, `hold` and `put` may be called without the big lock, as Linux reads a file under its inode's `i_rwsem` alone (mm/filemap.c:2614, fs/readdir.c:101). A reader holds its node's lock, which keeps the node's blocks and size, and reads blocks through the buffer cache, which has a lock of its own, or a file's data past it, as above. It looks a block up in the batch and copies its bytes under the batch's lists' lock (`ext2_writeback_copy`), with no big lock: a drain copies an image into the cache before it lets the image go, so a block is always in one of the two. A drain takes `em->batch` and the buffer locks, with no big lock; the error mark, a final put and the reap of dying inodes take the big lock (`ext2_mark_error`, `ext2_put`, `em_reap`), and a put that is not the last does not (`ext2_put_unless_last`). A file's size is stored under the node's lock with interrupts off across a count that is odd while it changes (`ext2_node_size_set`), and `getattr` reads it whole without the lock (`node_size`), as Linux's `i_size_write` and `i_size_read` do on a 32-bit machine (include/linux/fs.h:947-981). A lookup, a change of attributes and a sync of the volume run under the big lock still (`setattr`, `sync`); the operations on names do not, as Locks says.

The type sets `writes_unlocked` too, so `write`, `truncate`, `fallocate`, `fsync` and `fdatasync` of a regular file may be called without the big lock: the first three under the node's lock, as Linux's ext2 writes and truncates under `i_rwsem` (`generic_file_write_iter`, mm/filemap.c:4191-4206; `do_truncate`, fs/open.c:63-65; `ext2_setsize`, fs/ext2/inode.c:1274-1310), and the syncs under no lock of the node's, as `ext2_fsync` takes none (fs/ext2/file.c:153-163). The VFS reads an O_APPEND write's size and writes there under the node's lock, so two appends never meet, and cuts the pages mappings hold under it after a truncation (`file_write`, `ftruncate_fd`, syscall_fs.c). The buffer cache's write side has a lock of its own (bcache.h), so a writer stores into the cache, drains the batch and syncs without the big lock; it takes it only for a piece's commit, which checks the user's pages and copies from them (`writeback_try`, `vm_resident`), for the node table, the mount's error, a final put and a reap, and an fsync holds it across its look at the neighbours in the node's table block and its writes, which let it go at each wait (`file_sync`). Admission is counted under `em->admit_lock`, and the lists an fsync writes from under `em->share_lock`. A writer dirties a bitmap or a descriptor before it lists it, as Linux's `mark_buffer_dirty_inode` dirties a buffer before it lists it (fs/buffer.c), so an fsync that takes the list writes every block on it that a finished call dirtied, and lists it before the inode's store that names what it covers, so that the table block waits for it (`table_names`).

A lookup reads a directory's entries until it finds the name, as Linux's ext2 does. A hashed index (`dir_index`, `EXT2_INDEX_FL` in `i_flags`) is ignored, since its root lies in the slack of `..` and each of its blocks is one empty entry spanning the block, so the entries read as a linear directory. As in Linux's ext2, it starts at the block where the last lookup in that directory found its name, which the directory's node keeps under its lock as Linux keeps `i_dir_start_lookup` (fs/ext2/dir.c:356-395), and wraps around: `ls` and `find` look up names in listing order, so a listing takes time proportional to the names, not their square. A lookup and the searches that add and remove entries read each block once into a copy from the heap, or through the cache without one. `open` allocates a handle that holds the node until `release`. For a directory it keeps the last returned byte cursor and the directory's entry-change counter, which every entry change advances under the node lock, even when a write may fail after changing cached bytes. `readdir` returns every entry with an inode, `.` and `..` included, at a byte offset. On a counter mismatch or a seek, which may land at any byte of the directory (`dir_seeks_bytes`, [Readme.md](Readme.md)), it walks the current records from the start of the cursor's block to the first boundary at or past the cursor, as Linux's `ext2_validate_entry` and `ext2_readdir` do (fs/ext2/dir.c:236-258,288-297). Thus removal and reuse between calls cannot make it parse a new name as a header. A lookup first lets the directory lock go while it obtains the inode, so that a cold inode's read holds up no other lookup, and then checks the counter too, before rereading its saved entry offset. On a mismatch it searches once more and holds the lock until it has the node, as Linux holds the directory's `i_rwsem` across `ext2_lookup` and `ext2_iget` (fs/namei.c:1761, fs/ext2/namei.c:71), so changes of other names in a busy directory cannot send it round for ever; the node table's big lock comes after the node's lock, and the inode's load takes no node lock. `path` rebuilds a directory's path from `..` entries, for the `*at` calls. For a regular file, which has no `..`, it walks the directories from the root until it finds a name of the inode. The walk uses a heap stack bounded by the path capacity; it does not recurse on the kernel stack. An inode with more than one name gives the first name the walk finds. Executable descriptions instead keep the selected entry through the VFS name record, whose held parent directory has an unambiguous path; `/proc/PID/exe` follows that entry through rename and deletion and keeps the executable inode after unlink.

A device node has its numbers in the first pointer word, a byte each of major and minor, or in Linux's 32-bit encoding in the second when the first is zero. The driver's `open` answers `EACCES` for one; [open(2)](../../../man/man2/open.2) says what a program gets.

### Symbolic links

A target shorter than 60 bytes is kept in the inode's pointer words, with `i_blocks` counting no block but an extended-attribute block; a longer one is in the link's first block. `readlink` answers `EIO` for a size of a block or more, for a link of 60 bytes or more with no blocks, and for a long link without its block.

The resolver follows a link as [vfs.Readme.md](vfs.Readme.md) says. The calls that act on a final link itself are `lstat`, `lchown`, `readlink`, `unlink`, `rename`, `symlink` and `rmdir`; `link`, and `linkat` without `AT_SYMLINK_FOLLOW`; `fstatat`, `fchownat` and `utimensat` with `AT_SYMLINK_NOFOLLOW`; and `open` with `O_NOFOLLOW`, which answers `ELOOP`, with `O_PATH` and `O_NOFOLLOW`, which opens the link itself, or with `O_CREAT` and `O_EXCL`, which answers `EEXIST`. `stat`, `access`, `chdir`, `execve`, `truncate`, `chmod` and `chown` follow it.

### Locks

The driver's locks, outermost first. A lock is taken only while every lock held is of an earlier row, two locks of one class only at the subclass the lock-order checker is told, and each row follows Linux 6.12.115's ext2 and VFS; the kernel's whole order is in [the processes' Readme](../proc/Readme.md#locking).

| Row | Lock | Covers | Linux 6.12.115 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | the mount's rename lock, `vfs_mount.rename` | the ancestry of a directory moved to another parent, which `ext2_rename` and `is_below` read; the VFS takes it for a rename between two directories (`vfs_rename_lock`) | `s_vfs_rename_mutex`, taken by `lock_rename` (fs/namei.c:3250-3258) |
| 2 | a directory's node lock, `ext2 node` | its entries, size, block map and attributes; taken by a lookup, a listing and every change of its names. A rename's two directories go an ancestor before its descendant and others by inode number, the second at subclass 1 | the directory's `i_rwsem`, taken by `filename_create`, `do_rmdir` and `do_unlinkat` (fs/namei.c:4121, 4482, 4615) and for a rename in `lock_two_directories`' order (fs/namei.c:3215-3245) |
| 3 | any other node's lock, `ext2 node` | a file's data, size, block map and attributes; taken by a read, a write, a truncation, `fallocate` and a change of attributes, and for the node a name names in unlink, rmdir and rename, by inode number | `i_rwsem`: `generic_file_write_iter` (mm/filemap.c:4197), `do_truncate` (fs/open.c:63), held across `notify_change` (fs/attr.c:396) |
| 4 | the batch's lock, `em->batch`, `ext2 batch` | the file-write batch: the bytes of its images, its stage, a piece's plan and commit, and a drain; a strict mount holds it across its ordered writes | none: Linux writes a file's data into the page cache under `i_rwsem` and the folio's lock |
| 5 | a group's lock, `ext2 group`, one of `EXT2_GROUP_LOCKS` that the group numbers hash to | the group's block and inode bitmaps and its descriptor, in `em->gd`, in the cache and in the batch's images | `sb_bgl_lock` (fs/ext2/ext2.h:121-125, include/linux/blockgroup_lock.h:14-38), held to set or clear a bit (`ext2_set_bit_atomic`, fs/ext2/balloc.c:707, ialloc.c:486) and to change a descriptor's counts (balloc.c:175-178, ialloc.c:78-82 and 527-537) |
| 6 | the superblock's lock, `em->super`, `ext2 super` | a write of the superblock's free counts and state | `s_lock` over the superblock's buffer (fs/ext2/ext2.h:107-115), under which `ext2_sync_super` copies the counts into it (fs/ext2/super.c:1245-1260) |
| 7 | a buffer of the cache | its block, by the cache's rules (bcache.h) | the buffer's lock |
| 8 | the counts' lock, `em->count_lock`, a spinlock | the free counts, the allocation hint, the next generation, `s_state` and the feature words in memory | the free counts' `percpu_counter`s (balloc.c:1407, ialloc.c:523), `s_next_gen_lock` (fs/ext2/ext2.h:95, ialloc.c:564-566), `s_lock` for `s_mount_state` (super.c:58-61) |
| 8 | the batch's lists' lock, `wb->lock`, a spinlock | the blocks the batch has images of and their stages, and the inodes it holds | none |
| 8 | the admission lock, `em->admit_lock`, a spinlock | the count of admitted writers and the gate `closing` that a remount or an unmount shuts | `sb_start_write` and `sb_end_write`, the superblock's freeze counts (`s_writers`) |
| 8 | the fsync lists' lock, `em->share_lock`, a spinlock | each node's list of blocks for its fsync and the marks beside it (`shared`, `nshared`, `shared_over`, `writing`, `table_writing`, `unlisted`, `lost_list`, `dirty_meta`) and the mount's `shared_lost`, `device_lost` and `device_writes` | `i_private_lock`, which keeps a buffer's place on its inode's list (fs/buffer.c:475-486) |

Rows 1 to 6 are sleeping locks (`sleeplock.h`); the node locks and rows 4 to 6 count in their holder's `file_locks` (process.h), so that reclaim under them does not enter a file system. The four spinlocks are taken with interrupts off and never together, and nothing under them sleeps or takes a buffer; `em->share_lock` is taken under the buffer cache's write lock and its spinlock, in a sync's walk and a release (`table_names`), so nothing under it calls the cache. The node hash's chains change under it as well as the big lock, and a node leaves them before it is freed, so that the walk, which may run without the big lock, looks a node up under it alone.

A group's lock is a sleeping lock where Linux's is a spinlock: a bit is read and written through the cache, which may wait for a buffer another process holds, and a strict mount writes the bitmap and the descriptor through under it, where Linux sets a bit in a buffer it read before and writes it later. The group numbers hash to `EXT2_GROUP_LOCKS`, 64, by their low bits, as Linux hashes them to `NR_BG_LOCKS`, 128 at 32 processors or more. A holder waits on the disk only for a block evicted after its fetch: the bitmap and descriptor block of the group a search starts in are fetched before the lock is taken, and a search that meets another the cache lacks, or one another process holds, lets the lock go and fetches it or waits for it before anything has changed, as Allocation says. The process that plans marks itself, not the mount (`fs_plan` in process.h), so that plans in two groups run at once.

A caller holds one group's lock at a time, but for a batch's plan, which holds the locks of the groups its reserved blocks lie in, up to four, from the reservation until its commit, since a reserved block's bit is set only then; it holds `em->batch` meanwhile, so no two holders of several group locks wait on each other, and a lock two of its groups hash to is taken again by depth. The checker is told the second and later at subclasses 1 to 3.

The free counts change in the same step as the descriptor, under the group's lock, and `statfs` and the checks for `ENOSPC` read them under `em->count_lock`, as Linux reads its counters without the groups' locks. A write of the superblock takes `em->super`, then reads the counts and the state under `em->count_lock` and writes them, so that the last write carries the latest values and no older copy of the error bit or the counts reaches the disk after a newer one.

The batch's lists change only under both `em->batch` and `wb->lock`. An image is listed in the same step as its copy from the cache, with the buffer held, so that a later write through the cache to that block finds it. A caller without `em->batch` looks an image up and copies its bytes, in or out, in one step under `wb->lock`, so that a drain that empties the lists, or a plan that gives back its images, does not reuse a page under the copy.

The node table, its hash, the nodes' states and the reap list are kept by the big lock, and the hash's chains by `em->share_lock` too, as above. The buffer cache's dirty marks, writes and syncs are kept by its own write mutex, `wb_lock`, and its spinlock, `cache_spin` (bcache.h). The mutex is given up for requests and waits; a dirty-victim write needs no big lock, and neither does a write through the cache: `ext2_write_owned` copies into the block's buffer and into the batch's image of it while it holds the buffer, which a reader holds too (`ext2_read_bytes`), or the reader copies the image under `wb->lock`; each reader sees a write whole.

The operations on names, `create`, `mknod`, `unlink`, `mkdir`, `rmdir`, `link`, `symlink` and `rename`, run without the big lock, under the locks the VFS takes as Linux's does: the directory's for a new name or a removed one (`filename_create`, `do_rmdir` and `do_unlinkat`, fs/namei.c:4121, 4482 and 4615), the named node's in unlink, rmdir and rename (`vfs_unlink`, `vfs_rmdir` and `vfs_rename`, fs/namei.c:4553, 4424 and 5073-5079), and for a rename between two directories the mount's rename lock and then both directories in `lock_two_directories`' order (fs/namei.c:3215-3258). Each is admitted under `em->admit_lock` and lets the big lock go (`names_begin`): a caller that holds it, as a system call's router took it, has one hold let go and gets it back at the end, which is what a sleep in the operation did before, and one that holds it deeper keeps the rest. Under the directories' locks it searches the entries, decides, and changes the directory's node, as Linux's ext2 changes them under `i_rwsem` (fs/ext2/namei.c:102-404); it takes the big lock only for the node table, each time in one take: a node had or given back (`ext2_node_for`, `ext2_node_unhold`, `ext2_node_settle`), and a new inode allocated and published (`inode_new`), whose search skips a number the table still holds. A block mapped (`ext2_map_alloc`), an inode freed (`ext2_release_inode`), a strict mount's drain and its writes through the cache (`ext2_write_owned`, `ext2_inode_write`) take none, as a writer's of a file's data take none, nor does the check that a directory is not moved below itself (`is_below`), which reads copies of the ancestors' inodes and their `..` entries under the rename lock alone. The holds of a node whose last name goes are counted under the big lock in the step that frees it (`child_unlinked`), since only `ext2_node_for` gives a new hold on a node nothing else holds; so an open file's node keeps its inode until its last put, as before. These takes come after the node locks, which a waiter for one gives up as every sleep does, as the readers' takes do.

The name cache's `dcache_lock` (`dcache.c`) and the file page cache's `fc_lock` (`../mm/filecache.c`) are spinlocks of rows 10 and 9 of the kernel's order: the driver takes neither, and their holders make every call into it that may take a lock or sleep only with them let go, a lookup the cache misses (`dcache_lookup`) and a read of a file's pages into frames (`vfs_read_frames`) alike, so every lock above comes before them, as Linux takes `d_lock` and the `i_pages` lock under `i_rwsem` and never the other way (fs/dcache.c, mm/filemap.c:89-92). The one call under `dcache_lock` is `hold` (`ext2_hold`), from the cache's `keep` and `hit`, which raises a node's count atomically and takes no lock, as Linux's `dget_dlock` raises a dentry's count under `d_lock` (include/linux/dcache.h).

### Writing

The locks are in Locks above. The type record sets `file_locked`, so the VFS takes a node's lock, not the mount's, around a change ([vfs.Readme.md](vfs.Readme.md)), and the mount's namespace lock covers nothing here. A lookup, a listing, a read and `readlink` take the node's lock alone, and a read lets it go around a pointer block or a data block the cache lacks; a write, a truncation and a change of attributes take the file's, which the VFS holds too; and a namespace operation, which the VFS calls under the directory's lock, locks each node it reaches, the one node every holder of the inode shares and never a copy of it. `getattr` takes no lock, since a node changes only between sleeps. `sync`, `fsync` and `fdatasync` drain the batch under `em->batch`, and write the dirty buffers, the device's or one file's, and flush the disk with no lock held. So a slow request holds up only the callers that need the lock of the file it is for: `em->batch` and a group's lock wait on the disk only for a block fetched for them and evicted again, or on a strict mount for its ordered writes, and the blocks their holders read, a group's bitmaps and descriptors and the superblock, are fetched before they are taken. A block that another process holds, as a sync or an fsync holds each block for the length of its write, is waited for with those locks let go, by a plan, an allocation and the drain, as a block the cache lacks is fetched. `ext2_assert_node_locked`, `ext2_assert_batch_locked` and `ext2_assert_group_locked` panic on a change, a plan or an allocation made without its lock. `rmdir` keeps the removed directory's lock from its emptiness check until its link count is 0, and an entry is not added to a removed directory. A rename refuses a directory moved below itself, and one moved over a directory above its own parent, before it locks either node. A copy to or from a program's buffer is checked page by page for presence (`vm_resident`) in the step that copies, so that no fault comes under a node lock: a page that went is brought in with the locks let go, or, when the caller holds a file lock of its own, as `write` under the VFS does, ends the call short for the caller to bring it in. The lock-order checker gives a newly allocated inode subclass 4 while its parent is locked and before it has a directory entry; the published nodes use subclasses 0 through 3.

A mount is strict when it has `sync` or `dirsync` (`ext2_em_strict`). On a strict mount each metadata write reaches the medium before the next begins: `bcache_write` waits for the device and flushes the disk's cache. An inode bitmap alone goes by `bcache_write_unflushed`, and its descriptor's flush puts it on the medium. Any other mount writes back as Linux's ext2 does: a change of a directory's entries goes to the device before the call goes on, and any other change is dirty in the buffer cache and reaches the disk, in the order of the section below, at `sync`, `fsync` of the file, `syncfs`, unmount and eviction, and by the kernel's flusher once it has been dirty for `dirty_expire_centisecs` or the dirty buffers pass the background threshold, without the disk's flush ([Readme.md](Readme.md)); a writer past the dirty limit writes the device's dirty buffers in runs itself (`ext2_em_balance`). A run of writes shorter than the expiry period, such as a game's logs and caches while it loads, so reaches the disk only at a sync or past a threshold, as on Linux. On either kind of mount the superblock's free counts and state go straight to the medium (`ext2_sb_store`).

On a strict mount the writes reach the medium in this order:

* An inode is allocated by setting its bit and lowering its group's counts, then written initialised over its whole slot, then named; it is freed by freeing its blocks, then writing it zeroed with its deletion time, then dropping its reference to its extended-attribute block, then clearing its bit and raising the counts.
* A block is allocated in its bitmap and its group's count before a pointer points to it, and a new pointer block is written zeroed before the pointer to it. `i_blocks` goes with the pointers, and `i_size` last or with them.
* A directory marked indexed has `EXT2_INDEX_FL` cleared and its inode written before any of its entries changes, by an add, a remove, a rename over one of its names or a new `..` (`dir_settle`), as Linux's ext2 clears it in `ext2_set_link`, `ext2_add_link` and `ext2_delete_entry` (`fs/ext2/dir.c:468, 555, 609`): the driver keeps only the linear entries, and the index would go stale. `e2fsck -D` builds it again. A failed write of the inode, such as a failed read of the inode table, puts the flag back in memory and fails the change before any entry is written, so that the next change writes the cleared flag again first; the same step writes the inode of a directory whose growth failed to store it, below.
* A new entry goes into an empty record with room, or into the slack behind an entry's name, which the old record still covers, after which the old record is shortened to end where the new one begins; one write does both when the two headers and the new name lie in one 512-byte sector. A directory grows by a block zeroed with one empty entry, chained before or with the inode write that makes the size cover it; the entry comes last.
* An entry is removed before the link count drops: the first of a block by clearing its inode number, any other by joining its record to its predecessor's. The inode is freed with its last name; one that an open file or a working directory holds keeps its blocks until its last `put`, and a removed directory takes no new entry and lists as `ENOENT`.
* A truncation writes the smaller size and zeroes the tail of the last block kept, then clears pointers from the end, then frees the blocks. A growth writes the size over a hole.
* `mkdir` writes the new directory's block with `.` and `..`, then its inode with a count of two, then the parent's raised count, and the parent's entry last; `rmdir` removes the entry, lowers the parent's count and frees the inode. `link` raises the count, then writes the name; `symlink` writes a long target's block, then the inode, then the name, and keeps the link, as `create` and `mknod` keep theirs, when the directory's times then fail; `mknod` writes the inode with its device number, then the name.
* `rename` of a file raises its count, writes the new entry or points the destination's entry at it, its inode number and file type in one write of the entry's header as Linux's `ext2_set_link` sets both, removes the old entry and lowers the count, so the file has a name at every stop; a replaced file loses a count as in `unlink`. `rename` of a directory removes an empty destination as `rmdir` does, writes the new entry, rewrites `..` and raises the new parent's count when the parents differ, then removes the old entry and lowers the old parent's count. A move of a directory below itself is `EINVAL`, found by a walk up `..` before any write.

An inode's extended-attribute block (`i_file_acl`), which Linux and `mke2fs -d` write and several inodes may share, is counted in the inode's `i_blocks` and has a reference count in its header. Freeing the inode frees the block when the count is 1 and lowers the count otherwise, as Linux's ext2 does. A block out of range, or whose header lacks the magic number `0xEA020000` or a length of one block, is left as it is with the error bit set.

The `xattr` operation reads, lists, sets and removes `user.` attributes, as [getxattr(2)](../../../man/man2/getxattr.2) specifies. `ext2xattr.c` checks the whole external block before using an entry: the header and reference count, entry bounds and four-byte alignment, the index, name-length and name sort order, inline values within the block, and values outside the entries and one another. Other namespaces stay in a replacement block but are not exposed by these calls. In-inode attributes are preserved and not exposed. The codec writes Linux's name and value hashes and block hash; it does not consolidate identical blocks from different inodes.

Every attribute change builds a private copy of the block in memory. A block with one reference is then written in place, so a replacement that fits needs no free disk block; a shared block is replaced on disk. The node stays locked and the operation is admitted through the shutdown writer count. It drains the file-write batch, allocates a block when needed, and writes the device's dirty buffers and flushes before writing the attribute block durably. The superblock's `ext_attr` compatible feature is written before the inode, with a revision-zero superblock first promoted to revision one with its inode size and first unreserved inode. The inode's new `i_file_acl`, `i_blocks` and change time are then written durably. When the pointer changed, only then is the old block's reference released under the allocator lock, and the device written and flushed again. Removal of the last entry allocates no replacement. These barriers apply on a writeback mount too: a stop can leak a new block or leave the old reference count high, but cannot publish an uninitialised attribute block or free one an inode still names. A failed write returns its error and leaves uncertain allocations for e2fsck. A new block or a shared block's replacement needs a free block, and all entries and values must fit one file-system block.

Freeing a file's blocks (`ext2_punch`) runs in rounds of two phases: up to 512 pointers are cleared from the end and the inode stored, marked in the cache to reach the device first, and then those blocks are freed a group at a time, their bitmaps and descriptors marked to reach it after (`bcache_dirty_ordered`), so no pointer on the disk points to a block whose bit is clear, whichever path writes. A strict mount syncs after each phase. Each free keeps its list of blocks on its own stack, so two frees of two files that interleave at their disk waits keep the order by the cache's two classes alone.

A stop between two writes of a strict mount can leave an inode with no name (an orphan), a freed inode whose bit is set, a block nothing points to (a lost block), blocks past a file's size or an `i_blocks` behind its pointers, a link count above the names, an attribute block whose count is above the inodes that name it, or a directory with two names or a `..` naming the other parent. No data outside the range the operation was changing is lost or shared, and `e2fsck` repairs each. On a mount that writes back, a stop leaves whatever dirty buffers had reached the disk, in the order of writing back, for `e2fsck` to repair; it leaves no name on the disk for an inode freed or given to another file, so no entry of a removed name names a free inode or one of another type.

### The file-write batch

A write to a regular file goes through a batch the mount keeps across calls (`struct ext2_writeback`): private copies of the blocks it changes, a heap page each, 256 for data and 32 for metadata, taken when the mount becomes writable, and up to 16 pending inodes, which the batch holds. A batch takes at most 1 MiB of written bytes, a block written twice counting twice. A copy belongs to a stage: allocation bitmaps and descriptors; data; pointer blocks, a level at a time from those that point to data up; the superblock's `large_file` flag; inode table blocks.

The batch admits an overwrite of blocks the file has inside its size, and an append that starts on a block boundary or in a last block the file has; any other write drains the batch first and again after each block. Each block is planned before anything changes: the free block chosen and its bitmap and descriptor copied, the pointer path and data block copied or zeroed. Then it is committed, without I/O, to the copies and to the inode's pointers, `i_blocks`, size and times, which reads see at once, since `ext2_read_bytes` looks in the copies first. `ENOSPC` is decided before any of a write is accepted, from every data and pointer block the range lacks, against the free count less the reserved blocks for a caller that is not privileged (`proc_privileged`).

Each piece is planned and committed under `em->batch` and the locks of the groups it reserves blocks in, without a disk wait unless a block is evicted after its fetch, as an allocation is (Allocation below). The pointer path, a partial data block, the inode's table block and the bitmap and descriptor block of the allocator's group are fetched first with the lock let go; a plan that meets another block the cache lacks, or one another process holds, gives back its copies before the lock goes, since other writers change the tables and counts after it, and fetches the block or waits for it and starts again.

A drain writes the stages in order. On a mount that writes back it first has the cache write dirty buffers, with no lock held, until it can give one for each copy without a write (`bcache_reserve`), and then copies the stages into the cache under `em->batch`. A strict mount holds the lock across its ordered writes: each stage is synced and flushed before the next enters the cache. The batch drains when full, at `fsync` and `sync`, at the release of an open description, before a put of an unlinked inode, before a free of blocks of a file it holds, and on a strict mount before an attribute change or namespace operation (`ext2_assert_writable`), so no block is freed or reused while an older write is pending. Once the dirty buffers pass the dirty limit (`bcache_dirty_level`), a drain also writes the device's dirty buffers, without the disk's flush, but only for a process that has itself written 1 MiB since it last did (`ext2_em_balance`), as Linux's `balance_dirty_pages` throttles each writer by its own rate: a drain runs on whoever comes next, and a program that exits or logs a line pays nothing for what others dirtied. Below the limit the flusher writes back, and it drains a batch whose first byte is `dirty_expire_centisecs` old (`ext2_writeback_old`) and writes the cache back at once, so a pending write reaches the disk no later than a dirty buffer would.

On a mount that writes back, an attribute change or namespace operation leaves the batch pending. Every write into a block the batch has a copy of goes into the copy as well (`ext2_write_bytes`): an inode stored into a table block the batch holds, a bit beside its reservations, a descriptor, the superblock's counts. An inode the batch holds stays in memory, and its drain encodes it: its pointers may name blocks not yet in the cache, and the table block in the cache goes to the device with any neighbour's sync. A descriptor stored meanwhile counts the batch's blocks taken while the bitmap in the cache does not show them yet, so a stop then leaves a free count below the bitmap's; the check recounts it and `e2fsck` repairs it. A failed drain keeps its copies and resumes at its stage next time; a removed device is not tried again. A failure makes the mount read-only and sets its error bit, and the next `fsync`, `fdatasync` or `syncfs` of each description open before it, or of the first opened after it, reports it once ([vfs.Readme.md](vfs.Readme.md)). A write that fails after some of its bytes were accepted returns their count.

### The order of writing back

On a mount that writes back, whichever path writes, by a `sync`, an `fsync`, an eviction or the cache's reclaim, the blocks reach the device in this order:

* A change of a directory's entries is written to the device before the operation's next step, without the disk's flush (`dir_commit`), as Linux's `ext2_handle_dirsync` writes a directory's pages after every change of its entries with no test of `dirsync` (fs/ext2/dir.c:440-447, called at 470, 557, 611 and 650 in Linux 6.12.115): an entry added, removed or pointed at another inode, a new `..`, and a new directory block with its first entries. So a removed name is on the device before the inode it named loses its count and is freed or given to another file, and a renamed file's new name before its old one goes. Linux next writes the directory's inode (`sync_inode_metadata`); here its table block is written where a later step depends on it (`inode_commit`): a grown directory's new pointer, size and `i_blocks` before the entry in the new block, so that a rename's new name is reachable on the device before the old one goes, and when that write or the store before it fails, the directory stays marked (`store_pending`) and the next change of its entries writes the inode first, since the empty block inside the size in memory would take a retried rename's name with no store of its own; a cleared index flag before the entries change; and a new directory's inode, after its first block, before its parent names it, as `ext2_make_empty` writes it (fs/ext2/dir.c:650). The cache writes the table block after the dirty blocks it may name, as below: the shared ones, which a directory's own blocks were already among, and the blocks of a regular file beside it in the table block that allocated or freed since its last write, so such a write costs at most those files' new blocks and one request of its own. A change that leaves only the directory's times or link count writes them back in the order below, since a stop that loses them loses no name. A failed write fails the operation before its next step, and once a write of the device has failed the next one is `EROFS`, as on a strict mount.
* A block a directory or a symlink's target takes, and the pointer blocks above it, are written zeroed, marked `after`, to the device as soon as they are taken and before anything names them (`map_zero`, `shared_block_new`): the pointers and inodes a free cleared, which are `first`, reach the device before a block freed and taken again is overwritten there, and since no pointer block or table block waits for an `after` one, the write is not left to the order of writing back. A directory's block behind its direct pointers so costs one write more, its zeros alone, and a pointer block taken with it one of its own.
* A file's data and pointer blocks, and the bitmaps, descriptors and directory blocks, go before the inode table block that names them, and the table block before the `after` bitmaps and descriptors a free in it waits for. The constructor names the table blocks to the cache (`bcache_sync_last`), and the cache keeps a table block back until every block dirtied before its last change that it may name is written: a shared one, or a data or pointer block of an inode in it whose node has allocated or freed since its blocks were last written (`table_names`). A node freed with such a list sets `em->shared_lost`, and while that, or a write of the whole device that took it, is open, an inode not in memory counts as one that allocated, and so does one whose node was made since, which lists nothing (`lost_list`). A block a file overwrote in place is named on the device already, so a neighbour's overwrite does not wait for it or go with it.
* A data block goes before the pointer block that names it, and a pointer block before the one above it, at every depth, whether it is new or was on the device already. Each pointer block is dirtied with its level in the file's tree, 1 for one that names data (`bcache_dirty_pointer`), after the blocks below it: the batch's drain copies the data, then the pointer blocks a level at a time from the bottom, and `ext2_map_alloc` writes a block's zeros before the pointer to it. The cache keeps a pointer block back until every block of its owner below its level, dirtied before its last change, is written. A regular file's blocks are tagged with its inode, and a directory's are shared, as its entries are, so a directory's pointer block waits for the shared blocks.
* A failed write of a block keeps off the device every pointer block and table block that may name it and changed since it went dirty, and the `after` blocks behind those, until a retry writes the block. A `sync` stops at the failure; an eviction, a shrink and a reserve pass the waiting blocks over, and once a write of the device has failed they write no pointer block or table block at all.
* The cache holds a table block or a pointer block from the look that finds nothing left to wait for until its write ends, so an inode a drain stores while a sync sleeps waits for that write, and then for the data it names. What a block waits for was dirty before its last change, so it is written once at most before the block, and no block waits for ever: a reclaim passes it over and writes what it waits for, as other victims, a miss that finds no buffer it may write says so (`bcache: get: no free buffer`), and a sync writes both. On a write-through disk (`BLOCK_WRITE_THROUGH`), whose writes without the flush wait in its cache, the disk is flushed before a pointer block, a table block or an `after` block, so that what it waits for is on the medium first.

A sync of the device writes what was dirty when it began, the table blocks after every other block, so a steady writer does not keep it, and what it waits for is mostly written by then in runs. A bitmap that a free marked `after` keeps that place when an allocation sets a bit in it before it is written, so a stop then can find that bit behind the pointer to its block, which `e2fsck` repairs; no data is lost.

### One file's fsync

`fsync` and `fdatasync` of a regular file write that file and what it needs, not the device. A file's data and pointer blocks are dirtied tagged with its inode number (`bcache_dirty_owned`, and `bcache_dirty_pointer` for a pointer block that gains a pointer), by the batch's drain, the zeroing of a truncated tail and the pointers a free clears. A tag left on a block freed and given to another file costs one needless write. The blocks other files share that are dirtied for a file, the bitmaps and descriptor blocks of its allocations and frees and of its inode's allocation, and the superblock when `large_file` is set for it, are listed in its node, up to 8 (`n->shared[]`). A store of the inode that changes a field but its times sets `n->dirty_meta` under `em->share_lock` after the table buffer has been stored and dirtied, so a concurrent `fdatasync` cannot consume that store's mark before its bytes reach the cache. A failed store keeps the mark for any dirty buffer it left. A node also has the mark when it is made. The batch keeps changed inode fields in `wb->inode_dirty`, a bit per held node, across a failed drain, and publishes each mark after copying and dirtying its table image.

The call drains the batch, then, with no lock held, writes the listed blocks (`bcache_sync_block`), then the file's tagged blocks (`bcache_sync_owned`), then the inode's table block, and flushes the disk's cache. So a bit is set on the device before a pointer to its block, as a strict mount orders them, a bitmap a free dirtied `after` waits for the `first` pointers and inodes marked before it, whoever dirtied them, and with them for the blocks those table blocks name, and the table block itself waits for what it names, as the section above says. The table block holds other inodes too, as they are in memory, so each with a list of its own has that list and its tagged blocks written first, and a stop finds no neighbour's pointer to a free or unwritten block. These checks run again after every sleep, the batch drained among them, until the cache finds no wait or dependency for the table block (`bcache_sync_block_ready`). Writers can still change it before `bcache_sync_block`, so the cache checks dependencies again under its write lock with the buffer held until the write ends: a store before that hold has its dependencies checked, and one after waits for the hold. The big lock covers the node table during these checks, and does not exclude cache stores. Blocks taken off a list stay visible until they are on the device: the node counts those writes (`n->writing`), and another call on that file, or beside it in the table block, waits for them with no lock held before it goes on. A list a write of the whole device took is marked `n->unlisted`, and a list lost with its node `em->device_lost`, until that write ends; a call that finds either writes the whole device too, which waits for each buffer under write. `fdatasync` leaves out the table block while `n->dirty_meta` is clear and no other call is writing that block (`n->table_writing`): a change of times alone goes at the next `sync` or `fsync`. The whole device is written instead, as `sync` writes it, when the list overflowed, when a node with a list was freed since the last such write (`em->shared_lost`), when a neighbour in the table block with a list overflowed it or is not a regular file, or more keep being listed, for a file that is not regular, whose blocks are not tagged, and on a mount that is not writable. A write of the whole device empties every list, unless a batch is pending. A failure is recorded as the batch's is, before anyone waiting on that write wakes, and such a call returns it with its table block unwritten. It leaves the next call the whole device. A sync of the device writes the inode tables after every other block, whoever calls it, so a write that fails leaves them dirty, and a retry keeps that order.

A name is its directory's, so a new file needs an `fsync` of its directory, which writes the device, before a stop cannot lose it.

### The superblock's state

A writable mount clears the valid bit of `s_state` on the medium before the mount is published. The unmount's prepare step syncs the volume and sets the bit again only when every write succeeded, the error bit is clear and the mount's check was not stopped at a limit of its own (below), and clears it again if the VFS then abandons the unmount. A remount read-only does the same as the prepare step and makes the mount read-only under `em->super`, in the same hold as its write of the state. An error bit a reader sets while that write sleeps reaches the disk, as Linux's `ext2_error` writes it while a remount syncs: the state is written again, without the valid bit, while it changed during the write before, and the mount stops writing in one step under `em->count_lock` with the look that found it unchanged, so a bit set after that step was met on a read-only mount; a sync, which runs outside admission and may sleep on the superblock's block while the remount ends, looks again under that lock before it notes the superblock, and on a mount made read-only meanwhile writes nothing and records no failure, as Linux orders `sync_filesystem` and a remount by `s_umount`. A remount read-only is `EBUSY` while a file removed from the volume is open, since a read-only mount frees no inode. A remount either way first closes admission: every operation that changes the volume passes `em_enter` in the operations table, new ones sleep there, and the remount waits for those inside and for final puts, reaping what is left, before it scans for removed files. So an operation inside takes no lock past those it held when admitted, but a new or dying inode's, and a write, a truncation, a change of attributes and `fallocate`, which not every caller locks, take their file's lock before `em_enter`. A failed write, or a fault under `errors=remount-ro`, is no remount: it sets the mount read-only at once and waits for nobody. A remount takes `errors=` once admission is closed, so a second remount reads the policy the first left, and one without `errors=` keeps it. A shutdown of an ext2 volume, the root or another (`ext2_shutdown`, the `shutdown` of `vfs_ops`, which `kernel_shutdown` calls for each writable mount before scheduling stops) closes admission in the same way, after any remount under way, and never opens it again; it waits for those inside, holding no lock, then syncs and sets the valid bit as the prepare step does, so that no writer changes the volume after the bit is written. A writer in a disk wait that SIGKILL cannot end is the case it waits for. The wait has a deadline, 10 s from the volume's turn: a writer still inside then may hold `em->batch`, a group's lock, a node lock or a buffer on a disk that will not answer, so the shutdown writes nothing more and returns `EBUSY`, and the valid bit stays clear. A file removed but still open is `EBUSY` after a sync, as for a remount, since its inode would otherwise stay allocated on a volume marked valid. A volume cut off while writable, or whose writes failed, is therefore checked at its next writable mount. The superblock's free counts are the sums of the descriptors', which the driver keeps exact.

### The check

A writable mount or remount of a volume whose valid bit is clear or whose error bit is set checks it before writing. A read-only mount, asked for or forced by the format, is not checked and writes nothing. No mount that is not writable writes anything, as Linux's ext2 writes nothing to a read-only mount: outside the check and the opening of a writable mount or remount, `ext2_write_owned` refuses every write with `EROFS`, logged as `ext2: write refused: the mount is read-only: block B`. The one exception is a mount a fault made read-only under `errors=remount-ro`, whose batch still drains the file writes it accepted before, as Linux's flusher writes the dirty pages of a file system made read-only.

The check marks blocks in the bitmap the FAT drivers' checks share (`fat_walk_acquire` in `fatcommon.h`, [fat32.Readme.md](fat32.Readme.md)), one check at a time, and keeps on the heap one bit per inode, the inodes of 32 directory levels, an inode index with one allocated record per file with more than one link, a table of 512 attribute blocks whose count is above 1, and a bitmap block. It marks the structural blocks and those of the reserved inodes below `s_first_ino`; walks the tree from the root, marking each block every inode points to, its attribute block once however many inodes share it, and checking each directory's `.`, `..`, entries, holes and link count; reads the used inodes the walk did not reach; and compares both bitmaps with the marks, recounting each group's free counts into its descriptor.

It repairs nothing else. A used inode the walk did not reach is an orphan, its blocks marked so that nothing else takes them, unless its link count is 0, as a file removed while open leaves it at a stop, whose pointers are not read, as `e2fsck` reads such an inode; a used block nothing marked is lost, the blocks of such an orphan among them; and a shared attribute block whose count is above the inodes that name it is stale. All stay, held by their bits and counts, and the mount is writable with the error bit set, so that no unmount marks the volume clean before `e2fsck` has run; the log has `ext2: check: K attribute blocks with counts above their inodes, kept for e2fsck` when there are any, then `ext2: check: N lost blocks, M orphan inodes, kept for e2fsck` when there are any of those, or `ext2: check: clean`.

Whatever the check finds, the volume mounts writable, as Linux's ext2 mounts writable any volume whose features it supports, with a warning for a clear valid bit or a set error bit (`ext2_setup_super`); only the features above keep a volume read-only. A fault leaves the counts as the disk had them, sets the error bit, which the mount's write of the superblock puts on the disk, and is logged as `ext2: check: inode N: WHY`, then `ext2: mounting with errors, running e2fsck is recommended`: a block out of range or claimed twice; pointers that disagree with the size or `i_blocks`; an attribute block without its header, or named by more inodes than its count; a directory with two parents, a hole, or `.` and `..` out of place; an entry of the wrong form or type, or naming a free inode; a link count that disagrees with the names; or a bitmap that calls a used block or reached inode free. The check's own limits are no fault of the volume: a tree deeper than the levels, more shared attribute blocks than their table, or a workspace or hard-link record the heap cannot give stops the check, logged by name and then as `ext2: mounting unchecked, running e2fsck is recommended`, with the counts as the disk had them and the error bit as it was. Such a mount sets no valid bit at its unmount, as Linux puts back at unmount the state the volume had at mount (`ext2_put_super`), so the next writable mount checks the volume again.

### Allocation

A block is allocated first fit from the block after the last one given out, through every group once, ending at the start of the group it began in. The search takes each group's lock in turn and reads only what the cache has: the bitmap and descriptor block of the hint's group are fetched first with no lock held, and a search that meets another bitmap the cache lacks, or one another process holds, lets the lock go, fetches it or waits for it and starts again, before anything has changed. The eighth restart reads under the lock. An inode goes in its parent's group when that has one free, else in the next group that has, wrapping; its search runs the same way, with the bitmap and descriptor block of the parent's group fetched first, and restarts for another group's bitmap or descriptor block before the node is published. A caller that is not privileged cannot take the blocks the superblock reserves; no inodes are reserved. An operation that meets `ENOSPC` at its entry after allocating its inode frees the inode. A clear bit on a structural block, which only a damaged bitmap has, is never given out, by the file-write batch or by the allocator of the operations on names, attributes and directories: its bit is set, the counts are left, the fault is logged as `ext2: block B is free in its bitmap but holds the volume's tables` and reported as above, and the search goes on past it, as Linux's `ext2_new_blocks` refuses a block in the system zone (fs/ext2/balloc.c:1377-1392).

A new inode belongs to the caller's effective user and group, or to the directory's group when the directory is set-group-ID, and a new directory there is set-group-ID too, as Linux's `inode_init_owner` does. Its generation comes from a count that starts at each mount at the time in seconds shifted 8 bits left.

`cachestat` walks allocated branches of the inode's block map under its node lock, reading pointer blocks only from the write batch or idle resident buffers (`bcache_copy_cached`). A map branch that is not resident, or is busy, is skipped without disk I/O, and a page part of whose blocks lie under it does not count. A file page counts once when every allocated block of it is resident, with any pending or cached dirty block making the page dirty. Holes alone count nothing. The walk offers scheduling every 16 steps and keeps no buffer pointer across a scheduling point.

## Limits

* The largest file is Linux's `s_maxbytes` for ext2 (`ext2_max_size`): what the block pointers address, unless a dense file that large, its pointer blocks counted, would pass the 32-bit `i_blocks`, which caps it at 2196873666560 bytes at 4 KiB blocks. It also bounds `lseek` of a directory. A write that starts at or past it, a truncate that grows a file past it and an `fallocate` whose range ends past it are `EFBIG`, logged with the limit; as on Linux, a write that crosses it writes the bytes below it and returns their count, and a file already larger, which a volume written under a larger limit may hold, still shrinks.
* A directory is at most 4 GiB less a block (`EFBIG`), a link count 65000 (`EMLINK`), a name 255 bytes, and a symbolic link's target a block less one byte, 4095 bytes at 4 KiB blocks (`ENAMETOOLONG`).
* `path` and the rename check climb at most 2048 directories (`VFS_PATH_MAX / 2`).
* A 256-byte inode keeps each time to the nanosecond, from 1901-12-13 20:45:52 UTC to 2446-05-10 22:38:55 UTC. A new one has 32 extra bytes, or the superblock's wanted or minimum extra size when that is more, and its creation time is set, but no call reports it. A time an inode's `i_extra_isize` does not cover, and every time of a 128-byte inode, is signed 32-bit seconds, from 1901 to 2038-01-19 03:14:07 UTC, without fractional seconds. A time outside the range, supplied by a program or the clock, is clamped to the nearer limit.
* An `fsync` writes a file's inode with the 15 or 31 others of its table block, with the blocks of those that allocated since their last write, and a directory's `fsync` writes the whole device.
* Extended attributes are neither read nor written; of an attribute block the driver reads and writes only the header's magic number, length and reference count.
* Nodes in memory and mounts come from the heap without a fixed count (`ENOMEM`). There are no quotas.
* A lookup of a name the name cache does not hold and a change of attributes run under the big lock. Operations on names take it for the node table; a file's writes take it to copy from the user's buffer and for the node table. Reads, listings, the lookups the name cache answers and a file's writes, truncations, `fallocate` and syncs run without it outside the sections Locks names.
