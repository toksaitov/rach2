# ext2

`ext2.c` is the ext2 file system: the type `ext2` of `mount(2)` and the root of the installed system, read and written through the buffer cache (`bcache.h`). It exists so that the root on a disk has a standard format with a standard checker: the host's `mke2fs` makes the volumes (`tools/image/mkext2.sh`), and [e2fsck(8)](../../../man/man8/e2fsck.8) repairs them. The driver repairs nothing but free counts. It checks a volume not unmounted cleanly before writing to it, and on a `sync` or `dirsync` mount writes in an order that leaves every stop repairable without losing a file's data. The code is the same on i686 and x86-64.

## Interfaces

`ext2.h` declares one record, `vfs_ext2_type`: the name `ext2`, the constructor `ext2_mount`, the operations, `file_locked` and `name_cache` set, and `ns_serialised` and `nodev` clear (`vfs.h`). `mount(2)` finds it by name, and the boot path mounts an ext2 root with it through `vfs_init_root`, on the partition the boot line selects as [rach2/Readme.md](../../Readme.md) says. Of the VFS operations it leaves out `lookup_rest`, `pin` and `unpin`. The constructor ignores the option string. The driver reads three mount flags: `MS_RDONLY`, and `MS_SYNCHRONOUS` and `MS_DIRSYNC`, either of which makes the mount strict.

What a program sees is in the pages of the calls: [mount(2)](../../../man/man2/mount.2) for `sync`, `dirsync` and remount, [statfs(2)](../../../man/man2/statfs.2), [getdents64(2)](../../../man/man2/getdents64.2), [mknod(2)](../../../man/man2/mknod.2), [symlink(2)](../../../man/man2/symlink.2), [ioctl(2)](../../../man/man2/ioctl.2) for the inode flags, and [sync(2)](../../../man/man2/sync.2) for `fsync` and its recorded error. The kernel log has each mount as `ext2: mounted: N blocks of B, I inodes, G groups`, with `, read-only` for a read-only mount, and the records named below.

## Design

### The format

The driver takes revision 0 and 1 superblocks, blocks of 1024, 2048 and 4096 bytes, and inodes of 128 and 256 bytes. It reads the primary superblock at byte 1024 and the primary descriptor table at block `s_first_data_block + 1`, and never reads or writes the backup copies.

* Incompatible features: `filetype` is the one known; any other fails the mount, which excludes ext4's extents, 64-bit numbers and flexible groups, and a journal that needs recovery.
* Read-only-compatible features: `sparse_super`, `large_file` and `extra_isize` are known; any other mounts the volume read-only, and so does an `extra_isize` volume whose minimum extra size (`s_min_extra_isize`) is not a multiple of 4 or passes the inode.
* Compatible features: `dir_index` mounts the volume read-only, since a writer that keeps only the linear entries would leave the index stale. The rest are ignored, `has_journal` among them, so a journal is neither replayed nor written, except that the descriptor blocks `resize_inode` reserves must fit the volume.
* A block size below 4096 mounts the volume read-only: `bcache_write` writes a whole 4 KiB buffer, and two dependent metadata blocks in one buffer could not be written in order.

`tools/image/mkext2.sh` formats with 4 KiB blocks, 256-byte inodes, `sparse_super`, `large_file`, `extra_isize`, `filetype` and `resize_inode`, and gives every node the date of the checkout's commit as its four times. Images made before it have 128-byte inodes and no `extra_isize`, and mount as before.

The constructor fails with `EINVAL`, logged as `ext2: refused: WHAT`, when the magic is not `0xEF53` or the revision or sizes are not those above; the first data block is not 1 at 1 KiB blocks and 0 otherwise; a count is zero, out of range, or more than a bitmap block, the device or the groups hold; the descriptor table does not fit; a group's bitmaps or inode table lie outside it or overlap, or its free count passes its size; or the root inode is not a directory. A volume the driver mounts but does not write is logged as `ext2: mounted read-only: WHY`.

### Reading

An inode is the first 128 bytes of its slot and, in a 256-byte slot, the extra fields after them as far as `i_extra_isize` at byte 128 says: the extra words of the change, modification and access times at bytes 132, 136 and 140, and the creation time and its extra word at 144 and 148. An extra word has the nanoseconds in its high 30 bits and in its low two bits 32 and 33 of the seconds, added to the signed 32 bits of the low word, as Linux's ext4 has them. An `i_extra_isize` that is not a multiple of 4 or passes the slot makes the inode `EIO`, logged as `ext2: inode N: extra size X does not fit its 256 bytes`. The bytes past the creation time, in-inode extended attributes among them, are neither read nor written. The owner and group have their high 16 bits at bytes 120 and 122, and a regular file's size its high word at byte 108, so a regular file's size is 64 bits and a directory's 32.

Every number from the disk is checked before use. An inode number must be within the count. A block pointer must lie inside the volume and outside the structural blocks: the boot block, superblock copies, descriptor tables, bitmaps and inode tables (`data_block_ok`). A directory entry must lie inside its block and the directory's size, with a `rec_len` of at least 8 and a multiple of 4, its name inside the record and its inode within the count. A fault is `EIO` and a log record such as `ext2: inode N: block B is out of range`; a bad pointer or entry also sets the superblock's error bit, written at once on a writable mount, so that the next writable mount checks the volume.

An inode in use is a node in memory (`struct ext2_node`), from the heap, held by whatever uses it and freed at its last `put`; the name cache holds one for each name it keeps, thousands of them. A mount keeps its nodes on a list, for an unmount, and in a hash by inode number, for `node_for`: the number's low bits pick the bucket, so consecutive inodes have buckets of their own. The hash starts with 64 buckets in the mount's record and doubles from the heap whenever the nodes outnumber its buckets; a heap with no room leaves it as it is. `ext2_node_visits` in `/proc/stat` counts the nodes the searches read, [proc(5)](../../../man/man5/proc.5).

A node has a state. It enters the hash `N_LOADING`, before its inode is read or, for a new inode, in the step that picks the number, and goes `N_LIVE` once read or written. A second caller on a loading node takes a hold and sleeps on `node_wq`, so that one inode has one node; a load that fails makes it `N_FAILED`, wakes them with the failure, and the last hold frees it. References change between sleeps, with no lock. The last put of a linked inode frees the node in that step. The last put of an unlinked inode makes it `N_DYING`, which takes no new hold: a caller that finds one waits for it to go and looks again, which only a damaged volume makes anyone do. A caller with no file lock frees the inode there; any other leaves it on `em->reap`, since the free takes node locks out of order and waits on the disk, and it is done at the end of the operation, at the last `unlock_node` or `put`, or in `sync`, which every unmount passes. `inode_alloc` passes over a number in the hash, whose bit may be clear while it dies.

A file is read through 12 direct pointers and single, double and triple indirect blocks; a hole reads as zeros. A read does not change the access time.

A directory has no index, so a lookup reads entries until it finds the name. As in Linux's ext2, it starts where the last lookup in that directory found its name, remembered for 8 directories (`EXT2_LOOKUP_HINTS`), and wraps around: `ls` and `find` look up names in listing order, so a listing takes time proportional to the names, not their square. A lookup and the searches that add and remove entries read each block once into a copy from the heap, or through the cache without one. `readdir` returns every entry with an inode, `.` and `..` included, at a byte offset. `path` rebuilds a directory's path from `..` entries, for the `*at` calls. For a regular file, which has no `..`, it walks the directories from the root until it finds a name of the inode. The walk uses a heap stack bounded by the path capacity; it does not recurse on the kernel stack. An inode with more than one name gives the first name the walk finds. Executable descriptions instead keep the selected entry through the VFS name record, whose held parent directory has an unambiguous path; `/proc/PID/exe` follows that entry through rename and deletion and keeps the executable inode after unlink.

A device node has its numbers in the first pointer word, a byte each of major and minor, or in Linux's 32-bit encoding in the second when the first is zero. The driver's `open` answers `EACCES` for one; [open(2)](../../../man/man2/open.2) says what a program gets.

### Symbolic links

A target shorter than 60 bytes is kept in the inode's pointer words, with `i_blocks` counting no block but an extended-attribute block; a longer one is in the link's first block. `readlink` answers `EIO` for a size of a block or more, for a link of 60 bytes or more with no blocks, and for a long link without its block.

The resolver follows a link as [vfs.Readme.md](vfs.Readme.md) says. The calls that act on a final link itself are `lstat`, `lchown`, `readlink`, `unlink`, `rename`, `symlink` and `rmdir`; `link`, and `linkat` without `AT_SYMLINK_FOLLOW`; `fstatat`, `fchownat` and `utimensat` with `AT_SYMLINK_NOFOLLOW`; and `open` with `O_NOFOLLOW`, which answers `ELOOP`, with `O_PATH` and `O_NOFOLLOW`, which opens the link itself, or with `O_CREAT` and `O_EXCL`, which answers `EEXIST`. `stat`, `access`, `chdir`, `execve`, `truncate`, `chmod` and `chown` follow it.

### Writing

Each inode has a sleeping lock, and the mount has `em->meta` for the allocator and the batch; the comment at the top of `ext2.c` gives them, with the mount's rename lock, which the VFS takes for a directory's move, what each covers and the order they are taken in. The type record sets `file_locked`, so the VFS takes a node's lock, not the mount's, around a change ([vfs.Readme.md](vfs.Readme.md)), and the mount's namespace lock covers nothing here. A lookup, a listing, a read and `readlink` take the node's lock alone, and a read lets it go around a pointer block or a data block the cache lacks; a write, a truncation and a change of attributes take the file's, which the VFS holds too; and a namespace operation, which the VFS calls under the directory's lock, locks each node it reaches, the one node every holder of the inode shares and never a copy of it. `getattr` takes no lock, since a node changes only between sleeps. `sync`, `fsync` and `fdatasync` drain the batch under `em->meta`, and write the dirty buffers, the device's or one file's, and flush the disk with no lock held. So a slow request holds up only the callers that need the lock of the file it is for: `em->meta` waits on the disk only for a block fetched for it and evicted again, or on a strict mount for its ordered writes, and the blocks a holder of `em->meta` reads, a group's bitmaps and descriptors and the superblock, are fetched before it is taken. A block that another process holds, as a sync or an fsync holds each block for the length of its write, is waited for with `em->meta` let go, by a plan and by the drain, as a block the cache lacks is fetched. `assert_node_locked` and `assert_meta_locked` panic on a change or an allocation made without its lock. `rmdir` keeps the removed directory's lock from its emptiness check until its link count is 0, and an entry is not added to a removed directory. A rename refuses a directory moved below itself, and one moved over a directory above its own parent, before it locks either node. A copy to or from a program's buffer is checked page by page for presence (`vm_resident`) in the step that copies, so that no fault comes under a node lock: a page that went is brought in with the locks let go, or, when the caller holds a file lock of its own, as `write` under the VFS does, ends the call short for the caller to bring it in.

A mount is strict when it has `sync` or `dirsync` (`em_strict`). On a strict mount each metadata write reaches the medium before the next begins: `bcache_write` waits for the device and flushes the disk's cache. An inode bitmap alone goes by `bcache_write_unflushed`, and its descriptor's flush puts it on the medium. Any other mount writes back as Linux's ext2 does: a change is dirty in the buffer cache and reaches the disk, in the order of the section below, at `sync`, `fsync` of the file, `syncfs`, unmount, the periodic sync of [update(8)](../../../man/man8/update.8) and eviction, and in runs once a free or a drain leaves a quarter of the cache's limit dirty (`em_balance`). On either kind of mount the superblock's free counts and state go straight to the medium (`sb_store`).

On a strict mount the writes reach the medium in this order:

* An inode is allocated by setting its bit and lowering its group's counts, then written initialised over its whole slot, then named; it is freed by freeing its blocks, then writing it zeroed with its deletion time, then dropping its reference to its extended-attribute block, then clearing its bit and raising the counts.
* A block is allocated in its bitmap and its group's count before a pointer points to it, and a new pointer block is written zeroed before the pointer to it. `i_blocks` goes with the pointers, and `i_size` last or with them.
* A new entry goes into an empty record with room, or into the slack behind an entry's name, which the old record still covers, after which the old record is shortened to end where the new one begins; one write does both when the two headers and the new name lie in one 512-byte sector. A directory grows by a block zeroed with one empty entry, chained before or with the inode write that makes the size cover it; the entry comes last.
* An entry is removed before the link count drops: the first of a block by clearing its inode number, any other by joining its record to its predecessor's. The inode is freed with its last name; one that an open file or a working directory holds keeps its blocks until its last `put`, and a removed directory takes no new entry and lists as `ENOENT`.
* A truncation writes the smaller size and zeroes the tail of the last block kept, then clears pointers from the end, then frees the blocks. A growth writes the size over a hole.
* `mkdir` writes the new directory's block with `.` and `..`, then its inode with a count of two, then the parent's raised count, and the parent's entry last; `rmdir` removes the entry, lowers the parent's count and frees the inode. `link` raises the count, then writes the name; `symlink` writes a long target's block, then the inode, then the name; `mknod` writes the inode with its device number, then the name.
* `rename` of a file raises its count, writes the new entry or points the destination's entry at it, removes the old entry and lowers the count, so the file has a name at every stop; a replaced file loses a count as in `unlink`. `rename` of a directory removes an empty destination as `rmdir` does, writes the new entry, rewrites `..` and raises the new parent's count when the parents differ, then removes the old entry and lowers the old parent's count. A move of a directory below itself is `EINVAL`, found by a walk up `..` before any write.

An inode's extended-attribute block (`i_file_acl`), which Linux and `mke2fs -d` write and several inodes may share, is counted in the inode's `i_blocks` and has a reference count in its header. Freeing the inode frees the block when the count is 1 and lowers the count otherwise, as Linux's ext2 does. A block out of range, or whose header lacks the magic number `0xEA020000` or a length of one block, is left as it is with the error bit set.

Freeing a file's blocks (`punch`) runs in rounds of two phases: up to 512 pointers are cleared from the end and the inode stored, marked in the cache to reach the device first, and then those blocks are freed a group at a time, their bitmaps and descriptors marked to reach it after (`bcache_dirty_ordered`), so no pointer on the disk points to a block whose bit is clear, whichever path writes. A strict mount syncs after each phase. Each free keeps its list of blocks on its own stack, so two frees of two files that interleave at their disk waits keep the order by the cache's two classes alone.

A stop between two writes of a strict mount can leave an inode with no name (an orphan), a freed inode whose bit is set, a block nothing points to (a lost block), blocks past a file's size or an `i_blocks` behind its pointers, a link count above the names, an attribute block whose count is above the inodes that name it, or a directory with two names or a `..` naming the other parent. No data outside the range the operation was changing is lost or shared, and `e2fsck` repairs each. On a mount that writes back, a stop leaves whatever dirty buffers had reached the disk, for `e2fsck` to repair.

### The file-write batch

A write to a regular file goes through a batch the mount keeps across calls (`struct ext2_writeback`): private copies of the blocks it changes, a heap page each, 256 for data and 32 for metadata, taken when the mount becomes writable, and up to 16 pending inodes, which the batch holds. A batch takes at most 1 MiB of written bytes, a block written twice counting twice. A copy belongs to a stage: allocation bitmaps and descriptors; data; pointer blocks, a level at a time from those that point to data up; the superblock's `large_file` flag; inode table blocks.

The batch admits an overwrite of blocks the file has inside its size, and an append that starts on a block boundary or in a last block the file has; any other write drains the batch first and again after each block. Each block is planned before anything changes: the free block chosen and its bitmap and descriptor copied, the pointer path and data block copied or zeroed. Then it is committed, without I/O, to the copies and to the inode's pointers, `i_blocks`, size and times, which reads see at once, since `read_bytes` looks in the copies first. `ENOSPC` is decided before any of a write is accepted, from every data and pointer block the range lacks, against the free count less the reserved blocks for a caller that is not privileged (`proc_privileged`).

Each piece is planned and committed under `em->meta`, without a disk wait unless a block is evicted after its fetch, as an allocation is (Allocation below). The pointer path, a partial data block, the inode's table block and the bitmap and descriptor block of the allocator's group are fetched first with the lock let go; a plan that meets another block the cache lacks, or one another process holds, gives back its copies before the lock goes, since other writers change the tables and counts after it, and fetches the block or waits for it and starts again.

A drain writes the stages in order. On a mount that writes back it first has the cache write dirty buffers, with no lock held, until it can give one for each copy without a write (`bcache_reserve`), and then copies the stages into the cache under `em->meta`. A strict mount holds the lock across its ordered writes: each stage is synced and flushed before the next enters the cache. The batch drains when full, at `fsync` and `sync`, at the release of an open description, before a put of an unlinked inode, before a free of blocks of a file it holds, and on a strict mount before an attribute change or namespace operation (`assert_writable`), so no block is freed or reused while an older write is pending.

On a mount that writes back, an attribute change or namespace operation leaves the batch pending. Every write into a block the batch has a copy of goes into the copy as well (`write_bytes`): an inode stored into a table block the batch holds, a bit beside its reservations, a descriptor, the superblock's counts. An inode the batch holds stays in memory, and its drain encodes it: its pointers may name blocks not yet in the cache, and the table block in the cache goes to the device with any neighbour's sync. A descriptor stored meanwhile counts the batch's blocks taken while the bitmap in the cache does not show them yet, so a stop then leaves a free count below the bitmap's; the check recounts it and `e2fsck` repairs it. A failed drain keeps its copies and resumes at its stage next time; a removed device is not tried again. A failure makes the mount read-only and sets its error bit, and every later `fsync` reports the first one. A write that fails after some of its bytes were accepted returns their count.

### The order of writing back

On a mount that writes back, whichever path writes, by a `sync`, an `fsync`, an eviction or the cache's reclaim, the blocks reach the device in this order:

* A file's data and pointer blocks, and the bitmaps, descriptors and directory blocks, go before the inode table block that names them, and the table block before the `after` bitmaps and descriptors a free in it waits for. The constructor names the table blocks to the cache (`bcache_sync_last`), and the cache keeps a table block back until every block dirtied before its last change that it may name is written: a shared one, or a data or pointer block of an inode in it whose node has allocated or freed since its blocks were last written (`table_names`). A node freed with such a list sets `em->shared_lost`, and while that, or a write of the whole device that took it, is open, an inode not in memory counts as one that allocated, and so does one whose node was made since, which lists nothing (`lost_list`). A block a file overwrote in place is named on the device already, so a neighbour's overwrite does not wait for it or go with it.
* A data block goes before the pointer block that names it, and a pointer block before the one above it, at every depth, whether it is new or was on the device already. Each pointer block is dirtied with its level in the file's tree, 1 for one that names data (`bcache_dirty_pointer`), after the blocks below it: the batch's drain copies the data, then the pointer blocks a level at a time from the bottom, and `map_alloc` writes a block's zeros before the pointer to it. The cache keeps a pointer block back until every block of its owner below its level, dirtied before its last change, is written. A regular file's blocks are tagged with its inode, and a directory's are shared, as its entries are, so a directory's pointer block waits for the shared blocks.
* A failed write of a block keeps off the device every pointer block and table block that may name it and changed since it went dirty, and the `after` blocks behind those, until a retry writes the block. A `sync` stops at the failure; an eviction, a shrink and a reserve pass the waiting blocks over, and once a write of the device has failed they write no pointer block or table block at all.
* The cache holds a table block or a pointer block from the look that finds nothing left to wait for until its write ends, so an inode a drain stores while a sync sleeps waits for that write, and then for the data it names. What a block waits for was dirty before its last change, so it is written once at most before the block, and no block waits for ever: a reclaim passes it over and writes what it waits for, as other victims, a miss that finds no buffer it may write says so (`bcache: get: no free buffer`), and a sync writes both. On a write-through disk (`BLOCK_WRITE_THROUGH`), whose writes without the flush wait in its cache, the disk is flushed before a pointer block, a table block or an `after` block, so that what it waits for is on the medium first.

A sync of the device writes what was dirty when it began, the table blocks after every other block, so a steady writer does not keep it, and what it waits for is mostly written by then in runs. A bitmap that a free marked `after` keeps that place when an allocation sets a bit in it before it is written, so a stop then can find that bit behind the pointer to its block, which `e2fsck` repairs; no data is lost.

### One file's fsync

`fsync` and `fdatasync` of a regular file write that file and what it needs, not the device. A file's data and pointer blocks are dirtied tagged with its inode number (`bcache_dirty_owned`, and `bcache_dirty_pointer` for a pointer block that gains a pointer), by the batch's drain, the zeroing of a truncated tail and the pointers a free clears. A tag left on a block freed and given to another file costs one needless write. The blocks other files share that are dirtied for a file, the bitmaps and descriptor blocks of its allocations and frees and of its inode's allocation, and the superblock when `large_file` is set for it, are listed in its node, up to 8 (`n->shared[]`). A store of the inode that changes a field but its times sets `n->dirty_meta`, which a node also has when it is made.

The call drains the batch, then, with no lock held, writes the listed blocks (`bcache_sync_block`), then the file's tagged blocks (`bcache_sync_owned`), then the inode's table block, and flushes the disk's cache. So a bit is set on the device before a pointer to its block, as a strict mount orders them, a bitmap a free dirtied `after` waits for the `first` pointers and inodes marked before it, whoever dirtied them, and with them for the blocks those table blocks name, and the table block itself waits for what it names, as the section above says. The table block holds other inodes too, as they are in memory, so each with a list of its own has that list and its tagged blocks written first, and a stop finds no neighbour's pointer to a free or unwritten block. These checks run again after every sleep, the batch drained among them, and the last round sleeps nowhere: it ends in the step that takes the table block for its write (`bcache_sync_block_ready`), so that a change made during a wait goes first. Blocks taken off a list stay visible until they are on the device: the node counts those writes (`n->writing`), and another call on that file, or beside it in the table block, waits for them with no lock held before it goes on. A list a write of the whole device took is marked `n->unlisted`, and a list lost with its node `em->device_lost`, until that write ends; a call that finds either writes the whole device too, which waits for each buffer under write. `fdatasync` leaves out the table block while `n->dirty_meta` is clear and no other call is writing that block (`n->table_writing`): a change of times alone goes at the next `sync` or `fsync`. The whole device is written instead, as `sync` writes it, when the list overflowed, when a node with a list was freed since the last such write (`em->shared_lost`), when a neighbour in the table block with a list overflowed it or is not a regular file, or more keep being listed, for a file that is not regular, whose blocks are not tagged, and on a mount that is not writable. A write of the whole device empties every list, unless a batch is pending. A failure is recorded as the batch's is, before anyone waiting on that write wakes, and such a call returns it with its table block unwritten. It leaves the next call the whole device. A sync of the device writes the inode tables after every other block, whoever calls it, so a write that fails leaves them dirty, and a retry keeps that order.

A name is its directory's, so a new file needs an `fsync` of its directory, which writes the device, before a stop cannot lose it.

### The superblock's state

A writable mount clears the valid bit of `s_state` on the medium before the mount is published. The unmount's prepare step syncs the volume and sets the bit again only when every write succeeded and the error bit is clear, and clears it again if the VFS then abandons the unmount. A remount read-only does the same as the prepare step, and is `EBUSY` while a file removed from the volume is open, since a read-only mount frees no inode. A remount either way first closes admission: every operation that changes the volume passes `em_enter` in the operations table, new ones sleep there, and the remount waits for those inside and for final puts, reaping what is left, before it scans for removed files. So an operation inside takes no lock past those it held when admitted, but a new or dying inode's, and a write, a truncation, a change of attributes and `fallocate`, which not every caller locks, take their file's lock before `em_enter`. A failed write is no remount: it sets the mount read-only at once and waits for nobody. A shutdown of an ext2 root (`ext2_shutdown`, the `shutdown` of `vfs_ops`, which `kernel_shutdown` calls before scheduling stops) closes admission in the same way, after any remount under way, and never opens it again; it waits for those inside, holding no lock, then syncs and sets the valid bit as the prepare step does, so that no writer changes the volume after the bit is written. A writer in a disk wait that SIGKILL cannot end is the case it waits for. The wait has a deadline, 10 s from `kernel_shutdown`: a writer still inside then may hold `em->meta`, a node lock or a buffer on a disk that will not answer, so the shutdown writes nothing more and returns `EBUSY`, and the valid bit stays clear. A file removed but still open is `EBUSY` after a sync, as for a remount, since its inode would otherwise stay allocated on a volume marked valid. A volume cut off while writable, or whose writes failed, is therefore checked at its next writable mount. The superblock's free counts are the sums of the descriptors', which the driver keeps exact.

### The check

A writable mount or remount of a volume whose valid bit is clear or whose error bit is set checks it before writing. A read-only mount, asked for or forced by the format, is not checked and writes nothing.

The check marks blocks in the bitmap the FAT drivers' checks share (`fat_walk_acquire` in `fatcommon.h`, [fat32.Readme.md](fat32.Readme.md)), one check at a time, and keeps on the heap one bit per inode, the inodes of 32 directory levels, an inode index with one allocated record per file with more than one link, a table of 512 attribute blocks whose count is above 1, and a bitmap block. It marks the structural blocks and those of the reserved inodes below `s_first_ino`; walks the tree from the root, marking each block every inode points to, its attribute block once however many inodes share it, and checking each directory's `.`, `..`, entries, holes and link count; reads the used inodes the walk did not reach; and compares both bitmaps with the marks, recounting each group's free counts into its descriptor.

It repairs nothing else. A used inode the walk did not reach is an orphan, its blocks marked so that nothing else takes them; a used block nothing marked is lost; and a shared attribute block whose count is above the inodes that name it is stale. All stay, held by their bits and counts, and the mount is writable with the error bit set, so that no unmount marks the volume clean before `e2fsck` has run; the log has `ext2: check: K attribute blocks with counts above their inodes, kept for e2fsck` when there are any, then `ext2: check: N lost blocks, M orphan inodes, kept for e2fsck` when there are any of those, or `ext2: check: clean`.

A fault mounts the volume read-only, with the error bit written, the counts as the disk had them, and the first fault logged as `ext2: check: inode N: WHY`: a block out of range or claimed twice; pointers that disagree with the size or `i_blocks`; an attribute block without its header, or named by more inodes than its count; a directory with two parents, a hole, or `.` and `..` out of place; an entry of the wrong form or type, or naming a free inode; a link count that disagrees with the names; a bitmap that calls a used block or reached inode free; or a tree deeper than the levels or with more shared attribute blocks than their table. A workspace or hard-link record the heap cannot give mounts the volume read-only too, without marking capacity exhaustion as metadata corruption. Of the stops a strict mount leaves, a lost block, an orphan, a stale attribute block and a freed inode whose bit is set mount writable; the others mount read-only.

### Allocation

A block is allocated first fit from the block after the last one given out, through every group once, ending at the start of the group it began in. The search runs under `em->meta` and reads only what the cache has: the bitmap and descriptor block of the hint's group are fetched first with the lock let go, and a search that meets another bitmap the cache lacks, or one another process holds, lets the lock go, fetches it or waits for it and starts again, before anything has changed. The eighth restart reads under the lock. An inode goes in its parent's group when that has one free, else in the next group that has, wrapping; its search runs the same way, with the bitmap and descriptor block of the parent's group fetched first, and restarts for another group's bitmap or descriptor block before the node is published. A caller that is not privileged cannot take the blocks the superblock reserves; no inodes are reserved. An operation that meets `ENOSPC` at its entry after allocating its inode frees the inode.

A new inode belongs to the caller's effective user and group, or to the directory's group when the directory is set-group-ID, and a new directory there is set-group-ID too, as Linux's `inode_init_owner` does. Its generation comes from a count that starts at each mount at the time in seconds shifted 8 bits left.

## Limits

* The largest file is what the block pointers and the 32-bit `i_blocks` address: 2196875759616 bytes at 4 KiB blocks. A write, truncate or `fallocate` past it is `EFBIG`, logged with the limit.
* A directory is at most 4 GiB less a block (`EFBIG`), a link count 65000 (`EMLINK`), a name 255 bytes, and a symbolic link's target a block less one byte, 4095 bytes at 4 KiB blocks (`ENAMETOOLONG`).
* `path` and the rename check climb at most 2048 directories (`VFS_PATH_MAX / 2`).
* A 256-byte inode keeps each time to the nanosecond, from 1901-12-13 20:45:52 UTC to 2446-05-10 22:38:55 UTC. A new one has 32 extra bytes, or the superblock's wanted or minimum extra size when that is more, and its creation time is set, but no call reports it. A time an inode's `i_extra_isize` does not cover, and every time of a 128-byte inode, is signed 32-bit seconds, from 1901 to 2038-01-19 03:14:07 UTC, without fractional seconds. A time outside the range, supplied by a program or the clock, is clamped to the nearer limit.
* An `fsync` writes a file's inode with the 15 or 31 others of its table block, with the blocks of those that allocated since their last write, and a directory's `fsync` writes the whole device.
* Extended attributes are neither read nor written; of an attribute block the driver reads and writes only the header's magic number, length and reference count.
* Nodes in memory and mounts come from the heap without a fixed count (`ENOMEM`). There are no quotas.

## Tests

* `tests/run host` runs `tests/host/ext2_hosttest.c`, with the batch rows in `ext2_batch_cases.h` and `ext2_batch_lifecycle.h`, the rows of a node's life in `ext2_node_rows.h`, in which a second process runs inside the first's disk requests, the rows of the node locks in `ext2_lock_rows.h`, the rows of one file's `fsync` and `fdatasync` in `ext2_fsync_rows.h`, and the nested interleavings in `ext2_pair_rows.h`, and `tests/host/ext2_reserve_hosttest.c`, whose cache model checks the plans, an inode's search through groups the cache lacks, a block another process holds, which a plan and the drain wait for with `em->meta` let go, the drain's reserve and two interleaved frees under `em->meta`, and that a change of one file sleeps on its lock before admission. The first mounts, reads and writes volumes the host's `mke2fs` makes, sound and damaged, checks signed and clamped timestamps through a remount, with 128-byte inodes and with 256-byte inodes on an `extra_isize` volume, whose nanoseconds and times past 2038 must hold, requires 600 hard-linked files to pass a dirty mount and a wrong count in that volume to fail, and stops all but one of its write sequences after every write on a `dirsync` mount to judge the stop by `e2fsck` and by the check. The rows of the node locks take a page of a program's buffer away before the copy of a read and of a write, with and without a file lock held, remove a held directory before and after an entry is made in it, and rename into a directory's own subtree, over a directory above the source's parent, and over held files and directories. The interleavings run each pair of thirteen operations, a read, a write, an append, a truncation, a create, an unlink, an rmdir, a rename, a link, a change of attributes, an `fsync`, a `sync` and a last put, with the second run as another process at every disk request of the first: on different files in different directories it must end there, and on one file and one directory it must sleep on a node lock exactly when the first holds one it needs, and end otherwise; a rename between directories beside another must sleep on the rename lock. A sleep on the buffer of a block the request is moving passes too, and any other wait fails the row by its name (`--pairs-only` runs these alone). Among the rows of a node's life, a shutdown with a writer inside a disk wait must write the valid bit only once the writer has ended, and leave a volume `e2fsck -fn` calls clean; one whose writer's disk never answers must end at its deadline with the bit clear; and one with a removed file open must leave the bit clear (`--node-only` runs these rows alone).
* `tests/run ext2` runs file-system probes on an ext2 root and on a mounted volume, among them `ext2probe lookup`, a thousand lookups with the buffer cache full that read at most 2 nodes each by `ext2_node_visits`; boots os-real on an ext2 root through a kill, the check, a fault and recovery's `e2fsck`; reads one cold file under `rach2.blocklag` while a child's calls on another file of the same mount must go on, at least one round for each of the lag device's answers to the read (`lockwaitprobe lag`); and runs four writers in shared directories under memory pressure, after which the host's `e2fsck -fn` must find the volume clean (`lockwaitprobe stress`).
* `tests/run large` writes a file of 256 MiB, a mebibyte astride the 4 GiB line of a sparse file and a byte past the file limit, and `tests/run selfbuild-ext2` builds the kernel on an ext2 root.
