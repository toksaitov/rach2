# 80-96: write-back on Linux's policy

80-92 ([80-92.md](80-92.md)) found that in about half of Rach2's runs of Crysis's level load the data disk took 460 to 1,270 writes during the load, each with its flush, where Linux wrote twice, since its page cache holds dirty data for 30 s before writing it back. This task brings ext2's and the buffer cache's write-back to Linux's policy: dirty data waits for its age or the background threshold, a kernel thread writes it back, a writer past the dirty limit writes back itself, and only the syncs flush the disk's cache. 80-92 measures the load again once this and its three sibling tasks land.

## The cause

Two paths wrote during a load. update(8), which init started, called sync(2) every 30 s: it wrote every dirty buffer of every mount, with the order's flushes, and flushed each disk at the end, whatever the age of what it wrote. And a reclaim of a dirty buffer, at eviction, at a shrink and in `bcache_reserve`, which a drain of ext2's file-write batch calls to find a buffer for each of its copies, wrote the victim with the request flags 0, which on a write-through disk (AHCI, NVMe, a loop device) is a write and a flush: a flush for each 4 KiB block evicted. Linux's `pageout` writes a reclaimed page without one (mm/vmscan.c), and its flusher writes dirty pages back only once they are `dirty_expire_centisecs` old or dirty memory passes `dirty_background_ratio` (mm/page-writeback.c, fs/fs-writeback.c).

## The design

* The flusher (`rach2/sys/fs/writeback.c`) is a kernel thread, "writeback", that `start_processors` starts after the watchdog. Every `dirty_writeback_centisecs`, 5 s, it asks each mount's type to move into the buffer cache what it has kept pending for `dirty_expire_centisecs`, 30 s, or longer (the new `writeback` of `vfs_ops`), and writes back the cache's buffers dirty that long (`bcache_writeback`), without the disk's flush; once in each expiry period it writes the shared mappings' pages and the disk-backed window (`filemap_sync_all`, `pc_sync`), which keep no age, as update's sync did. Linux's `wb_workfn` with `wb_check_old_data_flush` does the same (fs/fs-writeback.c).
* The buffer cache keeps no clock: the host tests that link `bcache.c` have no tick. Its count of dirty marks, which already stamps each buffer with the count at which it went dirty, stands for time: the flusher reads the count with the tick and keeps 64 readings, and to write what has been dirty since tick T it writes the buffers dirty at or before the newest reading taken at or before T, never a newer one, so no buffer is written before its period. It reads the count at each turn, when the first dirty buffer of a clean cache wakes it, and a 32nd of the expiry period apart while any buffer is dirty, so a buffer waits at most that 32nd, under 1 s at the default, longer than on Linux, which stamps each inode with the time it went dirty.
* ext2's batch has a clock: `born` is the tick of its first byte since it was last empty, and `ext2_writeback_old` drains a batch that old. A drain by the flusher is followed at once by a write-back of the device's dirty buffers, those just drained among them, as Linux writes all of an expired inode's pages; otherwise the drained blocks would wait a second period in the cache.
* `bcache_dirty_level` places the dirty buffers against the background threshold and the dirty limit: `dirty_background_ratio`, 10, and `dirty_ratio`, 20, per cent of the cache's limit, or `dirty_background_bytes` and `dirty_bytes` when set, with the background threshold at half the limit when it is not below it, as Linux's `domain_dirty_limits`. Linux takes its ratios of the memory its page cache can grow into; here dirty data can grow only into the buffer cache, so the cache's limit is the base. A mark at or past the background threshold wakes the flusher, which writes back from its oldest reading forward until the cache is under it.
* Past the dirty limit a writer writes back itself, without the flush, as `balance_dirty_pages` holds a writer there: `ext2_em_balance`, at the end of a drain, writes the device's dirty buffers only for a process that has itself written 1 MiB since it last did, and does nothing below the limit. Before this task it ran `bcache_sync_unflushed` at a heaviness the cache judged by its own rule.
* A reclaim writes its victim without the flush (`reclaim_flags`), but a `first`, whose flush the order keeps; a block that waits for others has the disk flushed before it whatever wrote what it waits for (`write_follower`), so the order the marks promise holds on every path.
* update(8) is removed, from init, the images, the manual and the cases that read it; the machine case `sealed` reads `/proc/sys/vm/dirty_expire_centisecs` and `dirty_writeback_centisecs` in its place.

## Durability

The syncs are as they were, but for O_SYNC and O_DSYNC, which open(2) took and ignored, as its page said: update's 30 s sync was all that put such a write on the disk. A write on a description opened with either now ends with `fsync` or `fdatasync` of the file once its locks are let go (`write_synced`), as Linux's `generic_write_sync` syncs the range written, and a failed sync is the write's error; F_GETFL reports the two flags and F_SETFL leaves them, as Linux's. `fsync` and `fdatasync` drain the batch and write the file's buffers with `bcache_sync_owned` and flush; sync(2) and `syncfs` run each type's `sync`, `bcache_sync` and the disk's flush; `msync` with MS_SYNC writes the pages and syncs the file; an unmount and a clean shutdown prepare each volume as before. What the flusher has written already they find clean, and the flush at their end covers what it wrote without one. The dirty marks, the `first` and `after` classes, the pointer levels, `bcache_sync_last` and the batch's stage order are as they were, and the 84-85 lock design is untouched: the flusher takes admission and the big lock per mount as `vfs_sync_all` does, and the cache's write lock serializes its writes with every other. A stop of the machine loses at most what was dirtied in the last expiry period, the writeback interval and a 32nd of the period, 36 s at the defaults, against Linux's 35 s.

## /proc/sys/vm

Seven entries, as Linux has them, under the same names and with the same defaults and handlers ([proc(5)](../../man/man5/proc.5)):

| Entry | Default | Write |
|---|---|---|
| `dirty_background_bytes` | 0 | at least 1; clears `dirty_background_ratio` |
| `dirty_background_ratio` | 10 | 0 to 100; clears `dirty_background_bytes` |
| `dirty_bytes` | 0 | at least two pages; clears `dirty_ratio` |
| `dirty_ratio` | 20 | 0 to 100; clears `dirty_bytes` when it changes |
| `dirty_expire_centisecs` | 3000 | 0 or more |
| `dirty_writeback_centisecs` | 500 | 0 or more; 0 stops the periodic write-back |
| `dirtytime_expire_seconds` | 43200 | 0 or more; read by nothing, since no mount keeps times lazily |

A write of any of them wakes the flusher, which reads the settings at each turn.

## The check in QEMU

The i686 kernel and os-real image booted in QEMU with 512 MiB, two processors and a 128 MiB ext2 volume on AHCI, `/dev/disk0`, whose counters `/proc/diskstats` and `/proc/disk` gave around each step. The cache's limit there is 14,830 buffers, so the background threshold is some 5.8 MiB.

| Step | Writes | Sectors | Flushes |
|---|---|---|---|
| mount | 2 | 16 | 2 |
| a 4 MiB file, then 12 s | 2 | 16 | 2 |
| 30 s more | 10 | 8,240 | 10 |
| a 10 MiB file, then 7 s | 22 | 20,568 | 22 |
| a 1 MiB file and sync | 36 | 30,872 | 31 |
| umount | 40 | 30,904 | 35 |

The 4 MiB file stayed in memory with no write and no flush for 12 s and was on the disk 30 s later, in the turn after its blocks were 30 s old. The 10 MiB file passed the background threshold and the flusher wrote it back at once, and then nothing more. With `dirty_expire_centisecs` at 100, a 1 MiB file was on the disk between 3 s and 7 s after its write, the expiry and one writeback interval, as on Linux. The flushes that came with a write-back are the order's: each run of `first` buffers keeps its flush and each block that waits for others has one before it. Linux's ext2 keeps no order and sends none, but the order is ext2's guarantee here and stays; what the task removes is every write and flush that Linux's thresholds would not make.

A program built in the guest by TinyCC wrote 16 KiB in four writes: with no flag the disk took no write; with O_SYNC it took 19 writes and 11 flushes before the program ended, and with O_DSYNC 18 writes and 8 flushes, and F_GETFL gave 04010001 and 010001. The ps list showed the `writeback` thread and no update. Each kernel compiled for i686 and x86-64; the host tests that link the changed files (`ext2`, `ext2_reserve`, `bcache`, `bcache_cost`, `bcache_lock`, `fat32`, `exfat` and `synthfs`) compiled and linked, and `ext2_reserve`'s balance rows were changed for the levels. The harness's load is 80-92's to measure again.
