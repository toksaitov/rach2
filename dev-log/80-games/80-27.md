# WineD3D's GL round trip

Task 80-27 looks for why WineD3D's GL renderer took 871.9 ms under Rach2 for 1000 Direct3D 11 round trips on the Lenovo, against Linux's 239.1 ms on the same HD 4400 (80-5's Lenovo step). The same loop reproduces in QEMU on llvmpipe: 638 to 884 ms under Rach2 against 115 to 134 ms under Linux on the same processor, binaries and Mesa. There the time goes to the big lock: the loop's threads block and wake about 12,000 times, every block and wake runs under the one lock, and the lock is held for most of the loop with two or three processors waiting for it. One part of that cost, the interprocessor interrupt sent under the lock to wake a halted processor, is fixed here as Linux fixes it in a guest, with a poll before the halt; the loop then takes 494 to 605 ms. The rest is the big lock itself, which the owner kept for the next milestone (80-12). The Lenovo step, [80-27/lenovo.md](80-27/lenovo.md), measures the same split on the HD 4400; it waits for the manager's go. The figures below are from `80-27/evidence/`, measured on 2026-11-11 between 09:20 and 10:20 UTC+6 on the testing host.

## The loop

`tripprobe` (`tests/system/games/tripprobe.c`) clears a 64x64 render target, copies it to a staging texture and maps that, 1000 times. Under WineD3D, with its command stream thread, the program's thread queues each call and spins until the command thread has run it, calling `NtYieldExecution` in its spin: Wine's `getrusage(RUSAGE_THREAD)`, `sched_yield` and `getrusage` again (`ntdll.so` of GE-Proton11-12, read with objdump). The command thread runs the GL calls; on llvmpipe each map waits for the rasterizer's four threads, which a condition variable and a barrier start and stop. Under Linux, in the loop, each rasterizer thread blocked about 2,400 times and the command thread 1,630; under Rach2 the counts were the same, 2,082 to 2,567 and 2,091. The work is the same; its cost in the kernel is not.

## Measurements in QEMU

Rach2 booted 80-5's trial root, as 80-5's rehearsal does, on 4 processors under KVM, which on the testing host runs inside WSL's Hyper-V. Linux ran the same root's Proton, Mesa and Weston in a chroot on the host's kernel, held to 4 processors with `taskset`. `trip.py` read `/proc/stat` and the probe's threads when the probe's output gained its feature level line, just before the loop, and its result, just after.

| | Rach2, 80-5's kernel | Rach2, with the poll | Linux |
|---|---|---|---|
| Round trips, ms | 732.4, 647.3, 638.2; 884.2, 719.4, 718.2 | 556.2, 537.8, 494.3; 549.2, 540.8 | 120.8, 119.1, 128.1; 116.9, 115.6 |
| Big lock's waits over the loop, all processors | 1.74 s in 733 ms | 1.32 s in 541 ms | none |
| futex calls' holds of the lock | 9.5 to 11.0 us each, 28,309 to 31,194 | 6.9 to 7.0 us each, about 31,000 | none |

Without WineD3D's command thread (`WINE_D3D_CONFIG=renderer=gl,csmt=0x0`), and so without the spin, Rach2 took 656.9 and 525.7 ms against 1018.9 and 751.5 ms with it, run alternately, and Linux 156.1 and 159.9 ms; the lock still had 1.33 s of waits in 529 ms. The spin is not the main cost: the blocks and wakes are.

A static program, `80-27/evidence/pingpong.c`, gave the parts. A system call that waits for nothing costs Rach2 what it costs Linux, 0.26 us for `getpid` against 0.24. A wake of one thread that has slept 50 us, and its answer, took 10.09 us under Rach2 and 0.37 us under Linux, pinned apart or not. With QEMU's `-overcommit cpu-pm=on`, which keeps the guest's halts inside the guest, it took 4.34 us, but the loop took no less: 738.7 to 927.6 ms. Cycle counters put in a kernel for one run, and taken out, measured at 4,192 cycles a microsecond: an interprocessor interrupt's send took 26 us, a futex wake 3.0 us on average, nearly all of it those sends, a wait's work before it sleeps 0.34 us, and the switch's floating-point and debug-register part 0.16 us. On this host every interrupt sent and every halt left is a nested exit, so these parts cost there several times what they cost on a machine of its own.

Other work holds the lock too. Over one loop, 356 `getdents64` calls of another Wine process, which read the font directories, held it 272 us each, 97 ms in all; a tick that took the lock held it 40 us. Under Linux that work runs beside the loop.

## The fix

Linux sends no interrupt to wake a processor that polls its flags, and in a guest polls before it halts (cpuidle-haltpoll), for up to 200 us, a length that grows when a wake comes soon and shrinks when none does. Rach2's idle wait now does the same under a hypervisor (`idle_halt` in `rach2/sys/proc/sched.c`, [processes](../../rach2/sys/proc/Readme.md)): it polls its processor's `idle_poll` word with interrupts off and the big lock let go, answering other processors' requests, and a wake marks the word by compare-and-exchange in place of the interrupt (`smp_kick`). The one-thread wake fell to 4.16 us and the loop to 494 to 605 ms; the futex calls' holds to 7 us. On a machine of its own the wait halts at once, as before, since an interrupt and a halt cost a few microseconds there and a poll a busy processor's power.

## The remaining difference

What is left in QEMU is the big lock: about 31,000 futex calls, the halts' and wakes' switches, the ticks and the other processes' file work, each serialized, with two or three processors waiting at any moment. Linux takes the same calls under a lock of each futex bucket and each run queue. Removing the big lock from the futex and scheduler paths is the size of a milestone, and the owner kept the lock's removal for the next one (80-12). The Lenovo step reads the same counters on the HD 4400, where Crocus waits on the GPU's interrupt in place of llvmpipe's threads, together with each system's ioctls by request, to show how much of the 632.8 ms there is the lock and how much the Intel driver.
